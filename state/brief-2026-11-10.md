## Today's concept: Leasable vs. rentable vs. usable square feet

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you rent an apartment and pay for 900 square feet. But your actual bedroom, living room, and kitchen only add up to 820 square feet. The other 80 square feet is your share of the hallway, the stairwell, and the lobby — space you use but don't exclusively control. Your landlord split those common areas proportionally across every tenant and billed each of you for your slice.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, this split has three names with specific meanings.

**Usable square feet (USF)** — the space the tenant physically controls. Your actual office. Nobody else's.

**Rentable square feet (RSF)** — usable square feet plus the tenant's proportional share of common areas like hallways, restrooms, elevator lobbies, and mechanical rooms on that floor. This is what the lease is priced on.

**The load factor** (sometimes called the "add-on factor") is the multiplier that gets you from usable to rentable:

> Rentable SF = Usable SF × (1 + Load Factor)

Say Selerix Insurance Services — which just signed a 28,400 RSF office lease in Plano [REBusinessOnline] — has a load factor of 15% (typical for a mid-rise office building). Their actual usable space would be:

> 28,400 ÷ 1.15 = **~24,696 usable SF**

They're paying rent on 28,400, but their people only sit in roughly 24,700. The other ~3,700 square feet is their share of the building's common areas.

**Leasable square feet (LSF)** is the building owner's term — it's the total space in the building that *can* be leased, excluding permanently common areas like the main lobby or mechanical penthouse. It's the pool from which usable SF gets carved.

### 3. Why a professional cares

A tenant negotiating a lease needs to know the load factor before comparing two buildings — a 10,000 RSF suite in a building with a 20% load factor gives you 1,000 fewer actual desks than a 10,000 RSF suite with a 10% load factor, at the same stated rent. An investor underwriting (analyzing the financials of) an acquisition needs to know total leasable SF because that determines the maximum income the building can ever generate — you can't lease the elevator shaft.

## Check yourself

A law firm is choosing between two Plano offices. **Building A** quotes 5,000 RSF at $35/RSF per year, with a 20% load factor. **Building B** quotes 5,000 RSF at $35/RSF per year, with a 10% load factor. The annual rent is identical. Which building gives the firm more actual working space, and by how many square feet?

> ANSWER: Building B gives more actual working space. Building A: 5,000 ÷ 1.20 = ~4,167 usable SF. Building B: 5,000 ÷ 1.10 = ~4,545 usable SF. Building B delivers about 378 more usable square feet for the exact same rent — a meaningfully larger office at no extra cost.

## Review

**Development spread (Brief #36):** A developer builds an apartment complex and calculates that the total cost to build it — land, construction, fees — works out to $1,500 per square foot. The rents she expects to collect would, if she sold the building today, imply a value of $1,800 per square foot to a buyer. Without using the terms "development spread," "yield on cost," or "cap rate," explain what that $300 gap represents and why a developer wouldn't build if that gap shrank to zero.

> ANSWER: The $300 gap is the developer's profit — the difference between what it costs to create the building and what the market says it's worth once it's built. If that gap shrank to zero, the developer would spend $1,500 per square foot and end up with something worth $1,500 per square foot, meaning all the risk of construction was taken for no reward; no rational developer builds under those conditions. In professional terms, this gap is the development spread — the difference between yield on cost (the return based on what you spent) and the market cap rate (the return implied by what buyers will pay).

---

**Construction loan draw schedule (Brief #37):** A developer doesn't receive her entire $10 million construction loan on day one. Explain in plain terms how the money actually flows, and why a lender insists on doing it that way rather than handing over the full amount upfront.

> ANSWER: The lender releases money in stages — called draws — as specific construction milestones are completed and verified, so at any point the lender has only funded work that actually exists in the ground. If the developer stops building or defaults midway, the lender's exposure is limited to what's already been advanced, and there's a partially-built asset with real value securing the remaining loan rather than cash that may have been misused.
