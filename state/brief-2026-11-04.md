## Today's concept: Basis point

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine your savings account pays 4.00% interest today, and the bank raises it to 4.01%. That tiny move — one hundredth of one percent — is one basis point. Professionals use "basis point" (abbreviated bps, pronounced "bips") instead of saying "one hundredth of a percent" because it's faster and leaves no room for misunderstanding. One hundred basis points equals exactly 1.00%.

### 2. The real version

Yesterday the 10-year Treasury — the interest rate the U.S. government pays on 10-year loans, which acts as the benchmark for most real estate lending — was at 4.94%. Today it's at 4.91%. The move:

4.94% − 4.91% = 0.03% = **3 basis points**

That's not dramatic. But yesterday you learned that cap rates (the return a property produces, calculated as net operating income ÷ purchase price) tend to run about 150–200 bps above the 10-year. If the 10-year falls 3 bps, all else equal, cap rates could follow — meaning buyers might accept a slightly lower return, which pushes prices up. Even 3 bps matters when you're financing millions of dollars.

### 3. Why a professional cares

Lenders set loan rates in basis points above a benchmark, so a 25-bps move in the 10-year can mean tens of thousands of dollars of extra interest on a $5 million property loan. Investors also watch the spread between the 10-year and cap rates in bps — when that spread shrinks below roughly 150 bps, buyers are barely being paid for the extra risk of owning real estate versus a risk-free government bond, and deals start to stall.

## Review

**Migration and household formation (brief #43):** The "Sunbelt thesis" is an argument that certain Sun Belt cities are good bets for apartment investment. What are the two variables at the core of that thesis — the ones that drive apartment demand — and why do they matter to a property owner?

> ANSWER: The two variables are net migration (people moving into a metro from other states or countries) and household formation (those people splitting into separate households that each need a place to live); they matter because every new household is one more unit of rental demand, which fills vacancies and supports rent growth.

**Triple net vs. gross leases (brief #21):** A landlord is comparing two lease offers on the same retail space. Offer A is a gross lease at $3,000/month. Offer B is a triple net (NNN) lease at $2,200/month. Without knowing the actual expenses, which offer *could* be better for the landlord, and what do you need to know to be sure?

> ANSWER: The NNN lease could be better if property taxes, insurance, and maintenance together cost less than $800/month, because under a NNN lease the tenant pays those expenses on top of the $2,200 — but if expenses exceed $800/month, the gross lease at $3,000 puts more money in the landlord's pocket, since the landlord covers all expenses out of that flat payment.
