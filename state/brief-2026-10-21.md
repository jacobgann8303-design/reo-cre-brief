## Today's concept: Supply pipeline

### The everyday version

Imagine your apartment complex has one washing machine for 40 residents. There's always a wait. Now the landlord orders five more machines — but they won't arrive and be installed for eight months. For those eight months, nothing changes; the shortage is still real. Then all five machines arrive at once, and suddenly there's no wait at all. The number of machines "on order but not yet delivered" is the pipeline.

### The real version

In commercial real estate, the **supply pipeline** is the total amount of new space currently under construction or permitted — buildings that don't exist yet but are coming. It's measured in units (for apartments) or square feet (for offices and industrial).

Here's why it matters right now: CoStar reported this morning that multifamily starts (new apartment construction breaking ground) are **tumbling**. JLL says construction costs are rising **3–6% per year through 2027**. Put those together and the pipeline is shrinking — fewer new apartments are being ordered. That matters for MAA, which is up **1.39% today to $117.22**, because less future supply means the apartments MAA already owns face less competition for renters. Less competition → landlords can hold rents higher → more income → higher property values.

The arithmetic works like this: if a market has 10,000 existing apartments and 2,000 units in the pipeline, roughly 20% more supply is coming. If the pipeline shrinks to 500 units, that's only 5% more supply — a much softer headwind for existing owners.

### Why a professional cares

A developer deciding whether to break ground today won't see rent from those units for 18–24 months. If the pipeline is already heavy — lots of buildings already under construction — they know rents may be soft by the time their building opens. A thin pipeline is the green light; a fat pipeline is the warning.

## Check yourself

Houston's office market has a **25.6% vacancy rate** (meaning roughly one in four square feet sits empty) and leasing is slowing. If a developer is sitting on a fully permitted office project in Houston, what does a shrinking construction pipeline nationally tell them — and what does the local Houston data tell them? Do those two signals point in the same direction?

> ANSWER: The shrinking national pipeline is a positive signal — less future competition — but the local Houston data is a warning: 25.6% vacancy means there's already too much space, and slow leasing means demand isn't absorbing what exists. The two signals point in opposite directions, so a rational developer would weight the local data more heavily and probably hold off — adding supply into a market where existing space already can't fill up would likely push rents lower and leave the building partially empty.

## Review

**Percentage rent** (from brief #27): You own a retail strip center and your anchor tenant — a grocery store — signs a lease. The lease has a base rent of $8,000 a month, but it also says the tenant pays you extra once their annual sales cross a certain threshold. What is that extra payment called, and what's the landlord's basic motivation for including it?

> ANSWER: That extra payment is called percentage rent — it's a cut of the tenant's sales above a breakpoint written into the lease. The landlord includes it because it lets them share in the upside if the tenant does well, rather than locking in a fixed payment that might undervalue the space if the business booms.

**Ground lease** (from brief #35): A developer wants to build a 20-story apartment tower on a desirable piece of land downtown, but the landowner refuses to sell the dirt. They strike a deal anyway. What is that arrangement called, and who ends up owning what?

> ANSWER: That arrangement is a ground lease — the developer leases the land for a long period (often 50–99 years) and builds and owns the building on top of it, but the landowner keeps title to the dirt. When the lease eventually expires, the building typically reverts to the landowner.
