## Today's concept: Due diligence period

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you agree to buy a used car. You shake hands on $12,000, but before you hand over cash you want two weeks to take it to your mechanic, pull the accident history, and check whether the title is clean. During those two weeks the car is effectively yours to inspect — but you can walk away if your mechanic finds the engine is cracked. That window is your due diligence period.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, due diligence (sometimes called the "inspection period" or "DD period") is the stretch of time between signing a purchase contract and being legally obligated to close. The buyer pays a small deposit — called earnest money — to hold the deal. During due diligence they dig into everything: rent rolls (the list of every tenant, what they pay, and when their lease ends), environmental reports, structural inspections, loan documents, and the property's actual financials.

Here's why today's data makes this matter right now. CRE activity just fell 14% in October, partly because the 10-year Treasury is at **4.92%**. A buyer who signed a purchase contract three months ago — when rates were lower — might model their deal assuming a loan at, say, 6.5%. If rates have climbed since then and they can now only get 7.2%, their projected return drops. Due diligence gives them a defined window to re-run that math.

Simple version of the return shift: suppose a building produces **$300,000 per year in NOI** (Net Operating Income — revenue minus operating expenses, before debt payments). At a 6.5% loan rate the buyer's annual debt cost is manageable; at 7.2% it eats an extra $21,000/year on a $3 million loan ($3,000,000 × 0.007 = $21,000). That gap, discovered during DD, might kill the deal — or lead to a price renegotiation.

### 3. Why a professional cares

The length of the DD period is negotiated, and it's leverage. A seller wants it short (less time for the buyer to find problems or for the market to shift). A buyer wants it long (more time to uncover risk and arrange financing). In a market where rates are moving — like today, with the Fed signaling further hikes — a 60-day DD period is meaningfully more valuable than a 30-day one, because it gives you more time to lock a loan rate before it rises further.

## Check yourself

A buyer signs a contract to purchase a small office building for $2,000,000. During the 45-day due diligence period, their lender comes back and says the best available loan rate has jumped, adding $14,000/year in interest cost versus what the buyer originally modeled. The seller refuses to lower the price. The buyer's earnest money deposit is $40,000. If walking away costs them the $40,000 deposit but staying costs them $14,000/year in extra interest, how many years would it take for the extra interest cost to exceed the deposit loss — and what does that tell a buyer about whether to walk?

> ANSWER: $40,000 ÷ $14,000/year = 2.86 years, meaning if the buyer plans to hold the property more than about three years, the cumulative extra interest cost will exceed what they'd forfeit by walking away — giving them a rational financial reason to kill the deal and eat the deposit rather than close at the original price.

## Review

**Migration and household formation (brief #43):** The "Sunbelt thesis" is the idea that Sun Belt markets like Dallas, Austin, and Phoenix will keep growing because people are moving there from more expensive cities. Name the two variables that actually drive demand for apartment buildings in those markets, and explain in a sentence why each one matters.

> ANSWER: The two variables are net migration (people moving into a metro, which directly adds potential renters) and household formation (the rate at which people set up their own households rather than doubling up — a 25-year-old who moves to Dallas and rents their own apartment counts once for migration but also once for household formation, and both variables must be positive for apartment demand to grow).

**Class A / B / C (brief #55):** Someone tells you they just bought a "Class B" apartment building. Without knowing the city, can you say exactly how old the building is or what the rent is? Why or why not?

> ANSWER: No — Class A, B, and C are relative grades within a specific market, not absolute national standards; a Class B building in Lubbock might be newer and cheaper than a Class A building in Manhattan, so the label only tells you where the property sits relative to other buildings in that same city, not its absolute age, quality, or rent level.
