## Today's concept: Construction loan draw schedule

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine your parents are paying for your apartment renovation in stages. They don't hand the contractor $30,000 up front. Instead, they say: "Send us photos when the demo is done, and we'll send the first $8,000. Send photos when the drywall is up, and we'll send the next $10,000." The contractor only gets paid for work already finished and verified. This protects your parents from a contractor who takes the money and disappears.

### 2. The real version

A construction loan works the same way. A lender doesn't wire the full loan amount on day one — they release money in stages called **draws**, tied to verified milestones: foundation poured, framing complete, mechanical systems in, and so on. A third-party inspector (called a draw inspector or construction monitor) visits the site, confirms the work is done, and only then does the lender release the next chunk.

Here's why today's numbers make this tense. A construction loan on a $20 million project might carry a floating interest rate — meaning it moves with the market. If that rate is pegged to the 10-year Treasury, which is at **5.24% today**, and the developer projected 4.50% when they underwrote the deal, they're already paying significantly more per draw than planned. On a $20M loan, the difference between 4.50% and 5.24% on $10M drawn (halfway through construction) is roughly:

$10,000,000 × (5.24% − 4.50%) = $10,000,000 × 0.74% = **$74,000 per year in extra interest cost** — before the building earns a single dollar of rent.

That's money the developer didn't budget for, and it compounds as more draws get pulled.

### 3. Why a professional cares

A lender monitors every draw because releasing money too early — before the work is verified — means they're exposed if the developer runs out of money and walks away mid-construction. On the borrower's side, a developer who burns through early draws faster than planned, or faces rising rates mid-project, may hit a moment where they can't afford to keep drawing and the half-built building becomes a crisis — exactly the kind of situation showing up in that 11.49% CMBS special servicing rate today.

## Check yourself

A developer took out a $15 million construction loan with a floating rate currently at 5.24%. They've drawn $6 million so far. Their lender calls and says there's a problem: the draw inspector found that the framing work claimed in the last draw request wasn't actually complete — only about half was done. The lender says they'll only fund half of the $2 million draw the developer requested.

**What does the developer actually receive, and what is their annual interest cost on total drawn funds after this partial draw?**

> ANSWER: The developer receives $1 million (half of the $2 million requested), bringing total drawn funds to $7 million. Annual interest on $7 million at 5.24% = $7,000,000 × 0.0524 = $366,800 per year — and they still can't move to the next phase until the inspector signs off on the incomplete framing.

## Review

**Capital stack** (brief #13): A CRE deal was financed with $5M in senior debt (the first loan, paid back first if things go wrong), $1M in mezzanine debt (a loan that sits behind senior debt and gets paid second), $500K in preferred equity (investors who get a fixed return before common equity sees anything), and $1.5M in common equity (the developer's own stake, last to get paid, first to lose money). The deal goes bad and the property sells for only $5.8M. Walk through who gets paid and who doesn't.

> ANSWER: Senior debt ($5M) gets paid in full first; mezzanine debt ($1M) gets next claim but only $800K remains, so mezzanine lenders lose $200K; preferred equity and common equity get nothing. The higher up the stack, the safer your position — the lower down, the more risk and the more potential reward.

**Escalations and CPI bumps** (brief #24): A tenant signed a 5-year office lease at $30 per square foot per year, with a CPI bump — meaning rent increases each year by the same percentage as the Consumer Price Index (a government measure of how much everyday prices have risen). CPI came in at 3.7% this year (per today's headlines). What is the new rent per square foot for year two?

> ANSWER: $30 × 1.037 = **$31.11 per square foot** for year two. CPI bumps protect the landlord's purchasing power — without them, inflation silently erodes the real value of every rent check.
