## Today's concept: Free rent and effective rent

### The everyday version

Imagine a gym tries to get you to sign a year-long membership. To close the deal, they say: "First two months are on us — no charge." You still sign a twelve-month contract, but you only pay for ten. The posted price is $50 a month. But if you divide what you actually pay ($500 total) across all twelve months, your real cost is about $41.67 a month. The gym calls it "$50 a month." You're actually paying $41.67. Those two numbers are different, and both are true at the same time.

### The real version

That's exactly how free rent works in commercial leasing. A landlord signs a tenant to a five-year office lease at **$30 per square foot per year** on 10,000 square feet. To win the deal — especially in a soft market where office vacancy just came in at 17.2% nationally — the landlord offers **six months of free rent** up front.

Here's the arithmetic:

- Lease term: **60 months**
- Months tenant actually pays: **60 − 6 = 54 months**
- Annual rent: $30 × 10,000 sq ft = **$300,000/year**, or **$25,000/month**
- Total cash collected: 54 × $25,000 = **$1,350,000**
- Spread over 60 months: $1,350,000 ÷ 60 = **$22,500/month**
- **Effective rent: $22,500/month, or $27.00/sq ft/year**

The *face rent* (also called *asking rent* or *gross rent* — the number on the lease) is $30. The *effective rent* — what the landlord actually averages after the concession — is $27. Professionals sometimes call the gap between the two a *concession* or a *leasing incentive*.

### Why a professional cares

When you're comparing two deals — say, a tenant choosing between two buildings — face rent is misleading. A building offering $32/sq ft with eight months free might be cheaper in practice than one offering $29/sq ft with no free rent. Lenders and appraisers also care: a building full of leases with heavy free-rent concessions produces less actual cash in year one than the face rents suggest, which affects how much debt (the loan) the property can support.

## Check yourself

A landlord signs a 10-year lease at $40/sq ft/year on 5,000 square feet, with **12 months of free rent** at the start. What is the effective rent per square foot per year?

> ANSWER: The tenant pays for 120 − 12 = 108 months. Total cash = 108 × (($40 × 5,000) ÷ 12) = 108 × $16,667 = $1,800,036. Spread over 120 months: $1,800,036 ÷ 120 = $15,000/month, or $36,000/year ÷ 5,000 sq ft = **$36.00/sq ft/year effective rent** — $4 less than the face rent of $40.

## Review

**Debt service coverage ratio (Brief #57):** A lender is considering a loan on an apartment building. The building's NOI (Net Operating Income — the rent collected minus operating expenses, before the mortgage payment) is $180,000 per year. The annual mortgage payment would be $150,000. What is the DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio), and would a typical lender be comfortable with it?

> ANSWER: DSCR = NOI ÷ annual debt service = $180,000 ÷ $150,000 = **1.20x**. Most lenders require at least 1.20x to 1.25x, so this deal is right at the edge — a lender might approve it but would have little cushion if income dipped even slightly.

**ARGUS (Brief #46):** A job posting for a CRE analyst role lists "ARGUS proficiency required." In one sentence, what is ARGUS, and why do firms require it rather than just using a spreadsheet?

> ANSWER: ARGUS is specialized software that models a commercial property's cash flows lease by lease over time — handling things like expiration dates, renewal assumptions, and free-rent periods automatically — which is far harder to do reliably in a plain spreadsheet, especially on properties with dozens of tenants.
