## Today's concept: Loan-to-value and loan-to-cost

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you want to buy a used car that costs $20,000. You have $4,000 saved, so you ask the bank to lend you the other $16,000. The bank doesn't just hand that over — it asks: how much of this car's value are we on the hook for? In this case, they're lending $16,000 against a $20,000 car. That ratio — the loan divided by the thing's value — is what protects them if you stop paying and they have to sell the car to get their money back.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, that same ratio is called the **loan-to-value ratio**, or **LTV**. A lender takes the loan amount and divides it by the appraised value (what the property is worth today) of the property.

Say you're buying a small office building worth **$2,400,000** and you want to borrow **$1,680,000**.

**$1,680,000 ÷ $2,400,000 = 0.70, or 70% LTV.**

Most lenders today cap at 65–75% LTV on commercial deals. With the 10-year Treasury still at **4.77%**, lenders are already charging higher rates — so they're also being stricter about how much they'll lend against a property's value. Less loan relative to value means less risk for them if prices drop.

Now, **loan-to-cost**, or **LTC**, is a cousin metric used specifically when a building is being *constructed* rather than purchased. Instead of dividing by the market value, you divide by what it will actually cost to build.

If a developer plans to build a warehouse for **$3,000,000** in total construction costs and borrows **$2,100,000**:

**$2,100,000 ÷ $3,000,000 = 70% LTC.**

The distinction matters because a building under construction has no tenants yet and can't be sold easily — so lenders use cost (what's actually being spent) rather than a speculative finished value.

### 3. Why a professional cares

LTV sets the ceiling on how much debt a buyer can use — and because debt is usually cheaper than equity (the owner's own cash), a lower LTV limit means the deal needs more of the owner's money, which can kill a transaction or change who can afford to do it. During periods of high rates like right now, lenders often tighten LTV requirements at the same time rates rise, which squeezes deals from two directions at once.

## Check yourself

A developer is building a retail strip center. Total construction cost is $4,000,000. The lender will go up to 65% LTC. After the project is done, an appraiser says the finished building is worth $5,000,000. What is the maximum loan the developer can get under the LTC limit — and what is the resulting LTV once the project is complete?

> ANSWER: Maximum loan = 65% × $4,000,000 = $2,600,000. Once complete, LTV = $2,600,000 ÷ $5,000,000 = 52% — well inside a typical LTV limit, which is why lenders often use LTC during construction when the project hasn't been proven out yet.

## Review

**Basis points (Brief #6):** The 10-year Treasury moved from 4.79% to 4.77% between September 2 and September 3. Without using the words "percent" or "percentage points," how large was that move — and why do professionals express it that way instead of just saying "it fell 0.02%"?

> ANSWER: The move was 2 basis points (one basis point = one one-hundredth of a percentage point, so 0.02% = 2 bps). Professionals use basis points because in bond and lending math, tiny moves matter enormously at scale — saying "2 bps" is precise and unambiguous, whereas "0.02 percent" is easy to mishear or misrecord as "0.2 percent," a tenfold difference.

**Debt service coverage ratio (Brief #11):** A property generates $180,000 per year in NOI (net operating income — rental revenue minus operating expenses, before the mortgage payment). The annual mortgage payment is $150,000. What is the DSCR (debt service coverage ratio — the ratio of income to debt payment that lenders use to decide if a property can safely carry a loan), and would a typical lender making a commercial loan approve this deal?

> ANSWER: DSCR = $180,000 ÷ $150,000 = 1.20. Most commercial lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20 to 1.25, so this deal sits right at the edge — a lender requiring 1.25x would decline it, and any drop in NOI or rise in the loan payment at renewal would push it below the threshold.
