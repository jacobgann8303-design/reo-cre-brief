## Today's concept: IRR vs. equity multiple

### 1. The everyday version

Say you lend a friend $1,000, and two years later he pays you back $1,500. You doubled-and-a-half your money — your **equity multiple** (also called the multiple of invested capital, or MOIC) is 1.5x. Easy.

But now imagine a different friend: you give her $1,000, and she pays you back $1,500 — but it takes *ten years*. Same dollar amount. Same multiple. Very different deal, right? Your money was tied up for a decade instead of two years.

That's the gap the **Internal Rate of Return, or IRR**, fills. IRR is the annualized return that explains *how fast* your money grew. It accounts for the timing of every dollar in and every dollar out.

### 2. The real version

Take a simple deal. You invest $1,000,000 in equity (your share of the purchase price after the loan) in an apartment building today. You sell it in three years and get back $1,500,000 total — your original million plus $500,000 in profit.

**Equity multiple:** $1,500,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = **1.5x**. You got back one-and-a-half times what you put in.

**IRR:** You need to find the annual rate that turns $1,000,000 into $1,500,000 in exactly three years. The formula is:

$1,000,000 × (1 + IRR)³ = $1,500,000

Solving: (1 + IRR)³ = 1.5 → IRR ≈ **14.5% per year**

Now change just one thing: same $1,500,000 payout, but it takes *seven years* instead of three.

$1,000,000 × (1 + IRR)⁷ = $1,500,000 → IRR ≈ **6.0% per year**

Same 1.5x equity multiple. IRR drops from 14.5% to 6.0%. That's the whole point — the multiple doesn't tell you about time; IRR does.

And notice: today's 10-year Treasury is at **5.15%**. A 6.0% IRR on a seven-year hold barely clears the "risk-free" rate — the return you'd get just buying government bonds and doing nothing. That's why time destroys deals that look fine on paper.

### 3. Why a professional cares

An LP (limited partner — the passive investor who provides capital but doesn't run the deal) often has a **minimum IRR hurdle** — say, 8% — before they'll write a check. A deal can show a 2.0x equity multiple and still fail to clear that hurdle if the hold period is long enough. When a sponsor (the person running the deal) is pitching investors, they have to show *both* numbers — because one without the other is half the story.

## Check yourself

A deal returns a 2.0x equity multiple — you put in $500,000 and get back $1,000,000. Investor A holds for 4 years; Investor B holds for 10 years. Both have an 8% IRR hurdle — meaning they require at least 8% annualized return to invest. Without calculating the exact IRR, which investor's deal more likely clears the hurdle, and why?

> ANSWER: Investor A's deal more likely clears the 8% hurdle, because the same 2.0x return compressed into 4 years requires a much higher annualized rate than stretched over 10 years — a 2.0x over 4 years implies roughly 19% IRR, while a 2.0x over 10 years implies roughly 7.2% IRR, which falls below the 8% minimum.

## Review

**Review 1 — Anchor tenants and co-tenancy clauses (Brief #25):** A big-box retailer that anchors a strip mall closes its location. Three smaller tenants in the same center invoke something in their leases and reduce their rent by 30%. What is the clause they're using, and why did it exist in their leases in the first place?

> ANSWER: They are invoking a co-tenancy clause, which gives smaller tenants the right to reduce rent or even exit their lease if a named anchor tenant leaves — it exists because smaller tenants agreed to pay full rent partly on the assumption that the anchor's traffic would drive customers to them, so if that anchor disappears, the economic basis for their rent changes.

**Review 2 — Comparable sales / comps (Brief #40):** An appraiser is trying to value a 10,000-square-foot office building that sold last month. She finds a comp — a recently sold building nearby — but it's 15,000 square feet and sold 18 months ago. What are the two adjustments she almost certainly needs to make, and in which direction does each one go?

> ANSWER: She needs to adjust for size (the comp is larger, and larger buildings typically sell for less per square foot, so she likely adjusts the comp's price-per-square-foot upward to reflect the smaller subject property) and for time (the market may have shifted in 18 months, so she adjusts the comp's price to reflect current conditions — up if the market strengthened, down if it weakened).
