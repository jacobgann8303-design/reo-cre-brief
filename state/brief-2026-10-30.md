## Today's concept: Price per square foot

### The everyday version

Imagine you're apartment hunting. One place costs $900/month for 600 square feet. Another costs $1,050/month for 900 square feet. At first glance the second one looks more expensive — but divide the rent by the size and you get $1.50/sq ft versus $1.17/sq ft. Suddenly the bigger apartment is the better deal per unit of space. That's the whole idea: divide a dollar amount by square feet to get a number you can compare across very different-sized things.

### The real version

In commercial real estate, price per square foot (often written $/SF) is how brokers and investors compare buildings that are completely different sizes. Take today's headline: JLL sold a retail center in Birmingham for **$32.5 million**. The article doesn't give us the square footage of that specific deal, but let's use a round number to see the math. If that center were 200,000 square feet:

**$32,500,000 ÷ 200,000 SF = $162.50/SF**

Now imagine another retail center sells nearby for $140/SF. Without $/SF, you'd be comparing a $32M price tag to some other total dollar figure — meaningless. With $/SF, you immediately know which one the market valued higher per unit of space, and you can ask *why*.

For context: industrial warehouses (like the 1.1 million SF buildings SEG Solar just bought in Tomball, Texas) typically trade at much lower $/SF than retail or office, because warehouse space is cheap to build and less specialized.

### Why a professional cares

When a broker is pricing a building for sale, they pull recent $/SF sales of comparable properties — called "comps" — to anchor the asking price. If the market says retail in that submarket trades at $150–$170/SF and your building is 80,000 SF, you've got a rough value range of $12M–$13.6M before you even open a spreadsheet.

## Check yourself

A neighborhood retail strip center in Fort Worth is 45,000 square feet. A buyer pays $7,650,000 for it. A comparable center nearby recently sold for $185/SF. Was this a better or worse deal than the comp, on a per-square-foot basis?

> ANSWER: $7,650,000 ÷ 45,000 SF = $170/SF, which is below the comp's $185/SF, so the buyer paid less per square foot — a better deal for the buyer (they got it cheaper relative to what the market recently paid for similar space).

## Review

**FFO and AFFO** — REITs (companies that own income-producing real estate and trade like stocks) report something called FFO instead of regular net income. Without looking it up: what does FFO stand for, and in plain terms, why do REITs use it instead of the standard earnings number that other public companies report?

> ANSWER: FFO stands for Funds From Operations; REITs use it because standard accounting requires them to subtract depreciation (a paper reduction in the building's book value over time), but real estate often *appreciates* in value, so depreciation makes earnings look artificially low — FFO adds depreciation back to give a truer picture of the cash the REIT actually generates. AFFO goes one step further and also subtracts capital expenditures needed to maintain the properties.

**Replacement cost** — without looking it up, explain in one sentence what "replacement cost" means and why it acts as a ceiling on how high rents can go in a given market.

> ANSWER: Replacement cost is what it would cost to build an identical new building from scratch today; it acts as a rent ceiling because if existing rents rise high enough to make new construction pencil out (meaning the project earns a return), developers will build more supply — and that new supply competes with existing buildings and pushes rents back down.
