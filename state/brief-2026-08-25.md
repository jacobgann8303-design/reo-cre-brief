## Today's concept: Price per square foot

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you're comparing two apartments to rent. One is $1,200/month for 600 square feet. The other is $1,500/month for 900 square feet. Which is actually cheaper? You can't tell just from the rent. Divide price by size: the first is **$2.00 per square foot**, the second is **$1.67 per square foot**. The second apartment is cheaper per square foot even though its total rent is higher. Dividing by size puts them on the same scale so you can compare them fairly.

### 2. The real version

Commercial real estate works exactly the same way, just with bigger numbers. Today's headlines give us a real example: Tavaco Properties bought a 272,300-square-foot office building in Falls Church, Virginia for **$28.5 million**.

$28,500,000 ÷ 272,300 sq ft = **$104.67 per square foot**

That's the price per square foot — what the buyer effectively paid for each square foot of building. Now compare it to another headline: a New York investment firm paid to acquire a 757,000-square-foot industrial park in Houston's Greenspoint area. Industrial buildings typically trade at much lower prices per square foot than office buildings because they're simpler structures (concrete floors, big open bays, no fancy lobbies). If you saw the Houston deal price, you could divide and compare instantly — the price per square foot tells you whether that office building in Virginia was cheap or expensive relative to the market.

### 3. Why a professional cares

Price per square foot is how brokers, lenders, and appraisers do a quick sanity check — if a building is trading at $300/sq ft when everything else in that submarket (a specific geographic slice of a city) trades at $120/sq ft, something either unusual or wrong is going on, and you stop and ask why before any money moves.

## Check yourself

A developer is looking at two vacant office buildings in the same Dallas submarket (same neighborhood, same quality). Building A is 50,000 sq ft listed at $6.25 million. Building B is 80,000 sq ft listed at $9.2 million. Which one is cheaper on a per-square-foot basis, and by how much?

> ANSWER: Building A is $6,250,000 ÷ 50,000 = $125/sq ft; Building B is $9,200,000 ÷ 80,000 = $115/sq ft. Building B is cheaper by $10 per square foot, even though its total price tag is higher.

## Review

**Cap rate (Brief #1):** Without looking anything up — a property generates $180,000 per year in NOI (net operating income, meaning revenue after operating expenses but before loan payments) and is listed for $2,500,000. What is the cap rate, and does that number suggest the property is priced on the expensive side or the cheap side relative to a "typical" market?

> ANSWER: $180,000 ÷ $2,500,000 = 7.2% cap rate. A 7.2 cap sits in the middle range — lower cap rates (like 4 or 5) mean the buyer is paying more for each dollar of income, which signals an expensive or in-demand property; higher cap rates (like 8 or 9) mean the buyer is paying less, which signals either a cheaper market or more risk.

**NOI (Brief #2):** A small retail strip center collects $300,000 in annual rent. It pays $40,000 in property taxes, $18,000 in insurance, and $22,000 in maintenance. The owner also made a $60,000 roof replacement this year and has a $95,000 annual mortgage payment. What is the NOI?

> ANSWER: NOI = $300,000 − $40,000 − $18,000 − $22,000 = $220,000. The roof replacement (a capital expenditure — a one-time improvement to the physical asset) and the mortgage payment both sit below the NOI line and are excluded; NOI only captures recurring operating revenues and expenses.
