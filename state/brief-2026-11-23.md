## Today's concept: Offering memorandum

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you want to sell your car. Before a serious buyer commits, you hand them a packet: the Carfax report, maintenance records, photos, your asking price, and your argument for why the car is worth it. The buyer didn't see you drive it every day — that packet is their only window into what they're buying. If the packet is thin or sloppy, the buyer gets nervous and either lowballs you or walks.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, that packet is called an **offering memorandum**, or **OM** (sometimes also called a "deal book" or "pitch book"). When a property goes up for sale, the seller's broker assembles a document — typically 30 to 80 pages — that includes the property description, photos, market overview, tenant information, lease terms, and most importantly, the **pro forma** (the projected income and expense statement — you learned this Thursday). The OM presents an argument: *here's what the property earns, here's why it will keep earning, and here's the price that makes sense.*

Take today's headline: a REIT just sold a **396-unit apartment community in Virginia for $69 million**. Before any buyer bid on that deal, they received an OM. That OM would have shown the property's **NOI** (Net Operating Income — rent collected minus operating expenses, before debt payments) and implied a **cap rate** (NOI ÷ purchase price — the unleveraged yield a buyer gets on day one). If that community generated, say, $4.14M in NOI:

$4,140,000 ÷ $69,000,000 = **6.0% cap rate**

The OM made that case. Buyers used it to decide whether 6.0% was worth it at today's 4.92% 10-year Treasury.

### 3. Why a professional cares

The OM is the first filter in every acquisition process — if the numbers in it don't pencil, a buyer stops reading. The critical skill is knowing *which* assumptions to challenge: vacancy rates, rent growth, expense ratios. A seller's OM always tells the best version of the story, so a smart buyer's job is to find where the OM is optimistic.

## Check yourself

An OM for a small office building shows projected NOI of **$210,000** per year and an asking price of **$3,000,000**. The buyer's own research suggests the vacancy assumption in the OM is too low — real vacancy will reduce NOI by **$30,000**. What cap rate does the OM imply, and what cap rate does the buyer's adjusted NOI imply? Which number should the buyer use when deciding whether to make an offer?

> ANSWER: The OM implies $210,000 ÷ $3,000,000 = 7.0%. The buyer's adjusted NOI is $210,000 − $30,000 = $180,000, implying $180,000 ÷ $3,000,000 = 6.0%. The buyer should use the 6.0% figure — it reflects their own underwriting, not the seller's optimistic assumptions — and would likely argue for a lower purchase price if 6.0% is too thin at current rates.

## Review

**Absorption** (from brief #54): You're looking at a submarket — a defined chunk of a city's office market — with 500,000 square feet of total space. At the start of the quarter, 100,000 SF was vacant. By the end, 130,000 SF is vacant. Without knowing the definition, reason out: did the market absorb space or give it back? What do professionals call this condition, and what might it signal?

> ANSWER: The market gave space back — vacancy grew by 30,000 SF, meaning tenants vacated more than they leased. This is called negative absorption, and it signals weakening demand: more tenants are leaving or shrinking than new tenants are arriving to fill space.

**Percentage rent** (from brief #27): A retail landlord signs a lease with a coffee shop at $4,000/month base rent, plus 6% of gross sales above a "breakpoint" (the sales threshold where the percentage kicks in) of $800,000/year. In year one the coffee shop does $1,100,000 in sales. How much total rent does the landlord collect that year?

> ANSWER: Base rent is $4,000 × 12 = $48,000. Sales above the breakpoint are $1,100,000 − $800,000 = $300,000. Percentage rent is $300,000 × 6% = $18,000. Total rent = $48,000 + $18,000 = $66,000. Percentage rent gives the landlord upside when the tenant's business performs well.
