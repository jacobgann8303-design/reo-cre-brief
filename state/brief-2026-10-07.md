## Today's concept: 1031 exchange

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you bought a used car for $5,000, fixed it up, and now someone will pay you $12,000 for it. If you take the cash and walk away, you owe taxes on the $7,000 gain. But what if the IRS said: "We'll let you skip that tax bill — as long as you immediately roll that money into buying another car"? You defer the tax, your full $12,000 goes to work in the next vehicle, and you keep more buying power. That's the basic shape.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, Section 1031 of the tax code works exactly like that. If you sell a property and use the proceeds to buy another "like-kind" property — meaning another piece of real estate, residential or commercial — you can defer the capital gains tax that would otherwise be due.

Here's what that means in dollar terms. Say you bought a small apartment building for $1,000,000 and you're selling it today for $2,400,000. Your gain is $1,400,000. At a rough federal capital gains rate of 20%, you'd owe about **$280,000** in tax:

$1,400,000 × 0.20 = $280,000

Without a 1031 exchange, you have $2,120,000 left to reinvest. *With* a 1031 exchange, you roll the full **$2,400,000** into the next deal. That extra $280,000 stays invested and compounding.

The rules have teeth: you have **45 days** to identify your next property and **180 days** to close on it. And with the 10-year Treasury at **5.31%** right now — making debt expensive and deals harder to pencil — finding a replacement property you can actually afford within 45 days is genuinely stressful. That clock doesn't stop for market conditions.

### 3. Why a professional cares

A 1031 exchange changes whether a seller *will sell at all* — if they can't find a suitable replacement property in 45 days, they blow the exchange and owe the tax, which could wipe out a big chunk of their proceeds. In a high-rate environment like today's, where fewer deals make financial sense, the replacement property problem gets harder, and some sellers simply hold rather than risk triggering the tax.

## Check yourself

You own a warehouse you bought for $800,000. You sell it today for $2,000,000. Your capital gains tax rate is 20%. If you complete a 1031 exchange into a new industrial building, how much capital do you have working in the new deal — and how much more is that than if you had just paid the tax and reinvested the rest?

> ANSWER: Your gain is $1,200,000 ($2,000,000 − $800,000), and the tax would be $240,000 ($1,200,000 × 0.20). Without the exchange you'd reinvest $1,760,000; with the exchange you roll the full $2,000,000 — meaning $240,000 more is compounding in the new deal.

## Review

**Percentage rent** — taught a few weeks ago. A retail landlord leases space to a coffee shop. The base rent is $4,000/month. The lease also says the landlord gets 5% of any sales above $80,000/month. In a strong month the shop does $100,000 in sales. How much does the landlord collect total that month, and what is the piece above the base rent called?

> ANSWER: Sales above the $80,000 threshold are $20,000, and 5% of that is $1,000 — so the landlord collects $5,000 total ($4,000 base + $1,000 overage); that extra $1,000 is the percentage rent, the landlord's upside tied to the tenant's sales performance.

**Class A / B / C** — also from earlier in the semester. A broker tells you a building is "Class B." What does that actually mean, and why is it a relative label rather than a fixed standard?

> ANSWER: Class B means the building is a step below the newest, best-quality stock in that specific market — decent but not top-tier, usually older or with fewer amenities than Class A; it's relative because a Class A building in Lubbock might only be Class B in Houston, since the grade reflects local competition, not a national checklist.
