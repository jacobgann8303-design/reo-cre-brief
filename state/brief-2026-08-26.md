## Today's concept: The four asset classes

### 1. The everyday version

Think about the different ways people make money from physical stuff they own. Your landlord makes money from apartments — people need somewhere to sleep. The strip mall down the street makes money from stores — businesses need somewhere to sell things. The Amazon warehouse on the edge of town makes money because companies need somewhere to store and ship goods. The office park off the highway makes money because companies need somewhere to put their employees. Four completely different customers. Four completely different problems. Four completely different businesses — even though they all involve buildings.

### 2. The real version

Commercial real estate organizes itself into four main buckets — called **asset classes** — based on what the building is used for:

- **Multifamily** — apartment buildings. Tenants are people. Leases are short (usually 12 months). MAA, your REIT, owns these. It closed at **$132.69** today, up nearly 1%.
- **Office** — where companies put their workers. Leases are long (5–10 years). Today's headlines show a law firm just signed 107,000 square feet in D.C. — that's a big, long commitment.
- **Industrial** — warehouses, distribution centers, manufacturing plants. DFW and Houston just led the country in new industrial deliveries. Carrier may build a $433.8M plant in Fort Worth.
- **Retail** — stores and shopping centers. The Mesquite shopping center sale is a live example sitting in today's headlines.

Each class responds differently to the same economic conditions. The 10-year Treasury is at **4.70%** right now. That rate is a cost of borrowing. But it bites harder on office — where some buildings sit empty and can't easily refinance — than on industrial, where demand from e-commerce keeps rents high and vacancy low.

### 3. Why a professional cares

When a deal crosses your desk, the first question is: *which bucket is this?* The bucket tells you what risks to look for (Is retail dying in this zip code? Is remote work hollowing out the office?), what kind of tenant you're underwriting — that is, analyzing the financial reliability of — and how your financing will work. Getting the asset class wrong at the start of an analysis is like diagnosing the wrong disease.

## Check yourself

A developer friend tells you: "Industrial is crushing it in DFW right now, so I'm buying a retail strip center in Mesquite because real estate is hot." What's wrong with that reasoning, using what you know about asset classes?

> ANSWER: Each asset class responds to different demand drivers — industrial is booming because of e-commerce and supply chains, not because retail is strong; a healthy industrial market in the same city tells you almost nothing about whether retail tenants will fill that strip center or whether retail cap rates are favorable, so treating "real estate is hot" as one uniform fact is the mistake.

## Review

**Price per square foot** (Brief #3): A shopping center in Mesquite just sold. It's 167,000 square feet. If the sale price was $18.70 per square foot, what did it sell for — and what's one thing that number alone doesn't tell you about whether it was a good deal?

> ANSWER: 167,000 × $18.70 = $3,123,900; price per square foot tells you the size-adjusted cost but nothing about how much income the property actually generates — a building can look cheap per square foot and still be a bad deal if it's mostly vacant or the tenants are paying below-market rents.
