## Today's concept: REIT structure

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine ten friends want to invest in a rental house, but none of them can afford it alone. So they pool their money, buy the house together, and agree: any rent collected gets split among them every month, proportional to how much each person put in. One friend manages the property; the others are just along for the income. If the house rises in value, everyone benefits. If it falls, everyone shares the loss.

### 2. The real version

A REIT — Real Estate Investment Trust — works the same way, just at a massive scale and with publicly traded shares anyone can buy on the stock market. MAA (Mid-America Apartment Communities) owns roughly 100,000 apartment units across the Sun Belt. Instead of ten friends, there are thousands of shareholders. By law, a REIT must pay out at least 90% of its taxable income — the profit after expenses — as dividends (cash payments) to shareholders. That's the "split the rent" rule, enforced by the IRS.

Here's where today's numbers connect. MAA closed at **$114.83**, down **2.97%** in a single day. Why? The 10-year Treasury — think of it as the "safe" return the U.S. government pays you for lending it money — is at **5.29%**. If you can earn 5.29% just by buying a government bond with almost zero risk, you need MAA's dividend to look attractive by comparison. When the Treasury rate rises, investors demand a higher return from MAA too — and the way a stock's return goes up is its price goes down. That's the mechanical link. Professionals call this "yield compression working in reverse" — or more precisely, when rates rise, REIT prices fall so the dividend yield stays competitive.

Quick example: if MAA pays $4.56 per share annually in dividends and the stock is at $114.83:
**$4.56 ÷ $114.83 = 3.97% dividend yield.**
If the 10-year is at 5.29%, a 3.97% yield looks thin. Investors sell, price drops, and the yield rises until it feels worth the risk again.

### 3. Why a professional cares

When you're underwriting (analyzing) a REIT investment or advising a client who holds REIT shares, a rising 10-year Treasury is a direct headwind — it pushes REIT prices down even if the underlying buildings are performing fine. Today's MAA drop is a clean, live example: rates up 3 bps, REIT down 3%.

## Review

**Vacancy vs. availability (Brief #7):** A building's owner tells you it's "15% available" but only "9% vacant." You know these are two different numbers. Without looking anything up — what's the difference, and which one more accurately reflects how much space tenants are actually using right now?

> ANSWER: Vacant space is physically empty and not being paid for by any tenant — it's truly unoccupied. Available space includes vacant space plus space that is still occupied by a tenant (who is paying rent) but has been listed for lease because that tenant plans to leave or sublease it. Vacancy is the more accurate snapshot of space that isn't generating rent right now; availability is broader and captures future supply coming to market.

**Lease rollover risk and the maturity wall (Brief #26):** Imagine you own an office building where six of your ten tenants all signed 5-year leases in 2021. It's now 2026. What's the specific risk you face, and why does the current 10-year Treasury rate at 5.29% make that risk worse?

> ANSWER: All six leases expiring around the same time is lease rollover risk — you could lose most of your income at once if tenants don't renew, and you'd have to re-lease in whatever market conditions exist in 2026. The high 10-year rate makes it worse because tenants renewing or signing new leases know that financing anything costs more, so businesses are contracting space rather than expanding, meaning you have less negotiating leverage and may have to drop rents or offer costly concessions to keep tenants — right when your cash flow is most vulnerable.
