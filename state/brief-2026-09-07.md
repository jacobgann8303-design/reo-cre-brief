## Today's concept: Debt service coverage ratio

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you take home $3,000 a month and your car payment is $1,000. You're bringing in three times what you owe each month — comfortable. Now imagine your take-home drops to $900. Suddenly you can't cover the payment at all. A lender handing you a car loan cares deeply about that ratio: how much money do you make relative to what you owe each month? The bigger the cushion, the safer they feel.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, lenders ask the exact same question about a property. The ratio is called the **Debt Service Coverage Ratio**, or **DSCR** — it compares a property's NOI (Net Operating Income — the rent collected after paying operating expenses like insurance, taxes, and maintenance, but *before* paying the mortgage) to its annual debt service (the total mortgage payment for the year, principal plus interest).

The formula:

**DSCR = NOI ÷ Annual Debt Service**

Say an apartment building generates **$300,000 in NOI**. The mortgage payment is **$230,000 per year**.

$300,000 ÷ $230,000 = **1.30x**

That 1.30x means the property earns 30% more than it needs to cover the loan. Most lenders want to see at least **1.20x** — a 20% cushion — before they'll hand over money.

Now here's why today's data matters. The 10-year Treasury is sitting at **4.77%**. Commercial mortgage rates are typically priced *above* the 10-year (lenders want to be paid more than the "risk-free" government rate). That means a loan that cost a landlord $160,000 a year in debt service when rates were low might cost $230,000 or more today for the same loan amount — which is exactly why Texas multifamily loans are showing up in September foreclosures. The NOI didn't change; the debt service exploded.

### 3. Why a professional cares

A lender checks DSCR before approving any loan — if it's below their minimum (usually 1.20x), the deal doesn't get funded, period. An owner watches it constantly because if rents fall or expenses rise and DSCR drops below 1.00x, the property is literally not making enough to pay the bank, and default follows.

## Check yourself

A small office building has an NOI of $180,000 per year. A lender offers a loan that would require $160,000 in annual debt service. The lender's minimum DSCR is 1.20x. Does this deal get approved, and by how much does it clear (or miss) the threshold?

> ANSWER: DSCR = $180,000 ÷ $160,000 = 1.125x, which falls below the lender's 1.20x minimum, so the deal does not get approved as structured — the borrower would need to either increase NOI, reduce the loan amount (to lower debt service), or find a lender with a lower threshold.

## Review

**Class A / B / C (Brief #9):** You're looking at two office buildings across the street from each other in Dallas. One was built in 2022 with a rooftop terrace, floor-to-ceiling glass, and a full-service gym. The other was built in 1987, has decent bones, but the lobby is dated and there's no amenity package. Without knowing the rent, what class would you assign each — and is that classification permanent?

> ANSWER: The 2022 building would likely be Class A and the 1987 building Class B (or possibly C depending on condition) — but the classification is relative, not permanent; as new buildings enter the market, a Class A from 2022 can slip to Class B over time, and a renovated older building can move up.

**Leasable vs. rentable vs. usable square feet (Brief #10):** A tenant is quoted rent on 5,000 rentable square feet, but when they walk the space they can only put desks in about 4,350 square feet. What happened to the other 650 square feet, and what is the ratio of rentable to usable called?

> ANSWER: The extra 650 square feet is the tenant's share of common areas — hallways, lobbies, restrooms, stairwells — that they pay for even though they don't exclusively occupy them; the ratio of rentable to usable square feet is called the load factor (or add-on factor), and in this case it's 5,000 ÷ 4,350 ≈ 1.15, meaning a 15% load factor.
