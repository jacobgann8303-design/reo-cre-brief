## Today's concept: Common Area Maintenance (CAM) reconciliation

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you and three roommates split a house. In January, you each agree to pay $200/month toward shared bills — utilities, internet, lawn care. At the end of the year, your landlord adds up what those bills actually cost. If they totaled $10,800, you each owe $2,700 for the year. You already paid $2,400 ($200 × 12). So each of you owes a $300 **true-up** — the catch-up payment to cover the gap between what you estimated and what you actually spent.

### 2. The real version

In a commercial lease — say, a retail tenant at the Food Lion-anchored shopping center in Boone, NC that sold today for $26.1 million — the landlord estimates shared operating costs at the start of each year. These "common area maintenance" costs, called **CAM**, cover things like parking lot lighting, landscaping, snow removal, and roof repairs on shared spaces. The tenant pays a monthly CAM estimate on top of their base rent.

At year-end, the landlord does the **CAM reconciliation**: they total the actual costs, divide by the total square footage of the building, and multiply by the tenant's share.

Say a tenant occupies 10,000 SF out of 88,800 SF total (that's the Boone center's size). Their pro-rata share is:

**10,000 ÷ 88,800 = 11.26%**

If actual CAM costs for the year were $350,000, this tenant owes:

**$350,000 × 11.26% = $39,410**

If they already paid $36,000 in monthly CAM estimates ($3,000/month × 12), they owe a **true-up payment of $3,410**. If actual costs came in *lower* than estimated, the landlord sends the tenant a credit or refund instead.

### 3. Why a professional cares

When a lender is sizing a loan on that Boone shopping center, they look at how predictable the landlord's net income is — and big CAM true-ups that tenants dispute or can't pay are a red flag. For a tenant, an unexpected $30,000 CAM bill in February can blow up a small retailer's annual budget, which is why experienced tenants negotiate **CAM caps** (a ceiling on how much CAM can increase year over year) before they sign.

## Check yourself

A retail tenant leases 5,000 SF in a 50,000 SF strip center. The landlord estimated CAM at $4/SF for the year, so the tenant paid $20,000 in monthly installments ($4 × 5,000). At year-end reconciliation, actual CAM costs for the whole building totaled $240,000. Does the tenant owe more money, or do they get a credit — and how much?

> ANSWER: The tenant's pro-rata share is 5,000 ÷ 50,000 = 10%, so their share of actual costs is $240,000 × 10% = $24,000. They already paid $20,000, so they owe a true-up payment of $4,000 to the landlord.
