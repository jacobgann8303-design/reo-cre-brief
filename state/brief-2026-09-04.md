## Today's concept: Leasable vs. rentable vs. usable square feet

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you rent an apartment advertised as 900 square feet. But your actual bedroom, living room, kitchen, and bathroom only add up to 820 square feet. The other 80 square feet? It is your share of the hallway, the stairwell, and the lobby — space you use every day but do not have to yourself. You pay for all 900. The landlord advertised the bigger number.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate — offices, retail, industrial buildings — the same split exists, just with different names.

**Usable square feet (USF)** is the space that is actually yours: your office, your storage room, your four walls. No one else touches it.

**Rentable square feet (RSF)** is what you pay rent on. It is your usable space *plus* your share of common areas — hallways, lobbies, bathrooms, elevator banks, mechanical rooms. The landlord adds your share of those areas through a multiplier called the **load factor** (sometimes called the **common area factor** or **add-on factor**).

The arithmetic looks like this:

> RSF = USF × (1 + load factor)

A typical load factor in a downtown office building is around 15–20%. Say you need 5,000 usable square feet and the building has a 15% load factor:

> RSF = 5,000 × 1.15 = **5,750 rentable square feet**

If the rent is $40 per rentable square foot per year, you pay:

> 5,750 × $40 = **$230,000/year**

— not $200,000. That $30,000 gap is real money, and it is perfectly legal. Every lease in a multi-tenant building works this way.

**Leasable square feet** is the term used more often in retail. It refers to the total space in a shopping center that *can* be leased — it excludes things like management offices or common seating areas that are never rented to a tenant. You will see it reported as **GLA (gross leasable area)** in retail deals. When Bisnow reported the Chicago shopping center selling for $122 million, the price-per-square-foot math that analysts ran used GLA — not the building's total footprint.

### 3. Why a professional cares

When you are comparing two lease offers, the usable square footage tells you how much space your people actually get; the rentable square footage tells you what you actually pay for. A building with a 25% load factor might quote a lower price per rentable foot but deliver *less usable space* than a building with a 12% load factor at a higher headline rate. Running the comparison on rentable feet alone gets you to the wrong answer every time.

## Check yourself

A landlord quotes you two options for office space. Building A: 4,000 usable square feet, 20% load factor, $45/RSF/year. Building B: 4,000 usable square feet, 10% load factor, $50/RSF/year. Which building costs more per year, and by how much?

> ANSWER: Building A has RSF = 4,000 × 1.20 = 4,800, so annual rent = 4,800 × $45 = $216,000. Building B has RSF = 4,000 × 1.10 = 4,400, so annual rent = 4,400 × $50 = $220,000. Building B costs $4,000 more per year despite the lower load factor, because the higher per-foot rate more than offsets the smaller common-area charge — you must run both numbers, not just compare load factors or headline rates.

## Review

**Absorption (Brief #8):** A new office building delivers 200,000 square feet to the market in a quarter. Tenants sign leases for 120,000 square feet of it, but a tenant elsewhere in the same market vacates 50,000 square feet. What is net absorption for the quarter, and what does the sign of that number tell you?

> ANSWER: Net absorption = space newly occupied minus space newly vacated = 120,000 − 50,000 = 70,000 square feet positive; a positive number means the market is absorbing more space than it is giving back, which signals healthy demand — negative absorption would signal that tenants are shrinking or leaving faster than new ones are arriving.

**10-year Treasury and cap rate spread (Brief #5):** The 10-year Treasury has sat at 4.79% for two straight days. If a buyer was willing to accept a 150-basis-point spread (that is, 1.50 percentage points above the Treasury) to own an apartment building, what cap rate does that imply — and if the Treasury moved up to 5.29%, what would happen to property values, assuming the buyer still demands the same spread?

> ANSWER: At 4.79%, the implied cap rate is 4.79% + 1.50% = 6.29%; if the Treasury rose to 5.29% and the buyer still demanded a 150-bps spread, the required cap rate would jump to 6.79% — a higher cap rate means the same NOI (net operating income, the building's annual profit before debt payments) supports a lower price, so property values would fall.
