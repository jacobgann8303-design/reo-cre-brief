## Today's concept: Comparable sales (comps)

### 1. The everyday version

When your parents bought your childhood home, the realtor pulled up three or four nearby houses that had sold recently — same neighborhood, similar size, similar age — and said "houses like this one sold for around $280,000." Nobody invented that number. They found it by looking at what real buyers actually paid for real things that were close enough to compare. That's a comp. You're not guessing; you're anchoring to evidence.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, a **comparable sale** — pros say "comp" — is a recently sold property similar enough in type, size, location, and quality that its price per square foot (price divided by the building's square footage) or its **cap rate** (Net Operating Income ÷ sale price, where NOI is the building's annual rental income after operating expenses) tells you something reliable about what your property is worth today.

Here's the shape of it with real numbers. The GlobeSt headline today mentions a Chicago office tower — One North Wacker — trading for **$550 million**. That building is roughly 1.5 million square feet. So:

**$550,000,000 ÷ 1,500,000 SF = $367 per square foot**

If you're trying to value a different Chicago office tower of similar quality and location, $367/SF is now a data point — a comp. You don't know what your building is worth in isolation; you know it in relation to what buyers paid for something like it.

The 10-year Treasury at **5.10%** matters here because buyers use that rate as a baseline for the return they demand. When the 10-year is high, buyers want a higher cap rate, which means they pay less for the same income — and your comps from two years ago, when the 10-year was at 3.5%, are now stale and will mislead you.

### 3. Why a professional cares

A lender sizing a loan (deciding how much to lend) and a buyer setting an offer price both start with comps — if the most recent comparable sales show values falling, the lender lends less and the buyer bids lower. Stale comps from a lower-rate environment are one of the main reasons that one-in-four refinancing loans are falling short right now.

## Check yourself

A 200,000 SF office building in Dallas just traded for $48 million. A second Dallas office building — same submarket, similar age, similar quality — is listed for sale at $55 million and has 180,000 SF. Using the first sale as a comp, is the asking price on the second building above or below what the comp would suggest? Show the math.

> ANSWER: The comp sold at $48,000,000 ÷ 200,000 SF = $240 per square foot. Applying that to the second building: $240 × 180,000 SF = $43,200,000. The $55 million asking price is well above what the comp suggests — by about $11.8 million — meaning the seller is asking for a significant premium over recent market evidence.

## Review

**Lease rollover risk and the maturity wall** (from brief #26): A lender is evaluating a 150,000 SF office building. The owner has one tenant — a law firm on a single lease — and that lease expires in 14 months. The building's loan also matures in 18 months. Why does the combination of those two facts make this a riskier loan than if the lease ran another 10 years?

> ANSWER: Lease rollover risk is the danger that a tenant doesn't renew — leaving the building vacant and the income that supports the loan payment gone. Here, the lease expires before the loan matures, so the lender faces a scenario where the building could be empty when the owner needs to refinance; a vacant building generates no NOI (Net Operating Income), which means it can't support a new loan, and the borrower hits the maturity wall — the loan comes due with nowhere to go.

**Opportunity zones** (from brief #33): A developer is choosing between two otherwise identical parcels in Houston — one inside a federally designated Opportunity Zone, one just outside it. Both have the same construction costs and expected rents. What is the financial reason to prefer the one inside the Opportunity Zone, and who has to be involved for that benefit to matter?

> ANSWER: An Opportunity Zone is a low-income census tract where the federal government offers capital gains tax deferral and potential elimination on new investment, so a developer who raises equity (ownership money) from investors sitting on capital gains — say, from selling stock — can offer those investors a meaningful tax benefit that they can't get on the parcel outside the zone; the benefit only matters if the investor actually has capital gains to shelter, so the Opportunity Zone advantage is about attracting that specific type of equity capital.
