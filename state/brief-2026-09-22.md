## Today's concept: Tenant improvement allowance and leasing commissions

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you're renting an unfurnished apartment, but before you move in, the landlord offers to pay for new flooring and a fresh coat of paint — up to $3,000 — because the place needs work and they want a good tenant who'll sign a two-year lease. That $3,000 upfront cost to the landlord is an investment in getting you signed. And separately, if a leasing agent helped find you, the landlord pays that agent a fee too.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, those two costs have names. The **tenant improvement allowance (TI allowance)** is money the landlord gives the tenant to build out the space — new walls, flooring, lighting, whatever the tenant needs to actually operate. The **leasing commission (LC)** is what the landlord pays the brokers who put the deal together, typically 3–6% of the total lease value.

Here's what that looks like with real numbers. Say a tenant signs a 5-year lease at $40 per square foot per year for 5,000 square feet:

- Total lease value: $40 × 5,000 SF × 5 years = **$1,000,000**
- TI allowance: $50/SF × 5,000 SF = **$250,000** (landlord writes a check to the tenant to build out the space)
- Leasing commission at 4%: $1,000,000 × 4% = **$40,000**
- Total upfront cost to landlord before collecting a dollar of rent: **$290,000**

Now layer in today's market: with the 10-year Treasury at **5.01%**, that $290,000 the landlord is spending upfront costs more than it did two years ago — because the landlord either borrowed that money at today's rates, or gave up the chance to earn 5%+ on it elsewhere.

### 3. Why a professional cares

TI allowances and leasing commissions are the hidden cost of a lease — a deal that looks profitable on rent alone can look very different once you subtract $290,000 off the top. In a high-rate environment like today's, landlords try to reduce TI packages or push tenants to shorter buildouts, because every dollar spent upfront is a dollar that now has a 5% opportunity cost attached to it.

## Check yourself

A landlord signs a tenant to a 3-year lease: 2,000 square feet at $30/SF/year. The landlord offers a TI allowance of $40/SF and pays a leasing commission of 5% of total lease value. What is the landlord's total upfront cost before collecting any rent — and roughly how many months of rent does it take just to break even on those costs?

> ANSWER: Total lease value = $30 × 2,000 × 3 = $180,000. TI allowance = $40 × 2,000 = $80,000. Leasing commission = $180,000 × 5% = $9,000. Total upfront cost = $89,000. Monthly rent = ($30 × 2,000) ÷ 12 = $5,000/month. Break-even = $89,000 ÷ $5,000 = about 17.8 months — meaning the landlord doesn't see net positive cash flow until nearly halfway through the lease.

## Review

**IRR vs. equity multiple (Brief #15):** You invest $50,000 in a deal. Five years later you get back $100,000 — a 2.0x equity multiple (meaning you doubled your money). Your friend invested $500,000 in a different deal, got back $800,000 in the same five years — a 1.6x multiple. Who had the better IRR (internal rate of return — the annualized percentage return on your invested dollars), and why can a great IRR still mean a smaller dollar gain?

> ANSWER: Your $50,000 → $100,000 deal likely has the higher IRR because you doubled your money on a smaller base in the same timeframe; IRR measures the annualized rate of return on each dollar invested, not the total dollars earned — your friend made $300,000 in profit versus your $50,000, so a great IRR can still mean a much smaller check if the amount invested was small.

**Due diligence period (Brief #20):** You're under contract to buy a small office building. You have a 45-day due diligence period. On day 10, your inspector finds that the HVAC (heating and cooling) system needs $80,000 in repairs — something the seller didn't disclose. What is the due diligence period actually protecting you from, and what are your realistic options at that moment?

> ANSWER: The due diligence period is a contractually protected window during which you can investigate the property and back out (usually with your deposit returned) if you find problems — it protects you from being locked into a bad deal; at day 10 with $80,000 in repairs found, your realistic options are to renegotiate the price down, ask the seller to fix it before closing, or walk away entirely without penalty since you're still inside the window.
