## Today's concept: FFO and AFFO

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you own a food truck. Last year it made $30,000 in profit — but $8,000 of that is what your accountant calls "depreciation" on the truck itself. That means accounting rules say the truck lost $8,000 in value just by existing another year, so they subtract it from your profit. But the truck still runs fine. You didn't actually spend that $8,000. Your real cash in pocket was $30,000, not $22,000.

Now say you also had to replace the fryer mid-year for $2,000. That was a real cash expense that depreciation accounting didn't fully capture. So your *true* cash picture sits somewhere in between.

### 2. The real version

REITs — like MAA, which closed today at $117.65 — own buildings that accountants say lose value every year through depreciation (the same concept as the truck). That makes GAAP net income (GAAP = Generally Accepted Accounting Principles, the standard accounting rulebook) look smaller than the actual cash the business generated.

So the industry uses two adjusted numbers:

**FFO (Funds From Operations):** Take GAAP net income, add back depreciation, and add back any losses from selling properties.

Say MAA reports net income of $200M, with $150M in depreciation added back: **$200M + $150M = $350M FFO.**

**AFFO (Adjusted Funds From Operations):** Take FFO and also subtract the real cash spent on maintaining existing properties — things like replacing HVAC units and repaving parking lots. If maintaining MAA's portfolio costs $40M: **$350M − $40M = $310M AFFO.**

AFFO is the truer number for "how much cash did this business actually generate?"

With the 10-year at 5.28%, investors use AFFO to calculate whether MAA's dividend yield (the dividend paid out divided by the stock price) beats or at least competes with a nearly risk-free 5.28% Treasury — which is the tension introduced last brief.

### 3. Why a professional cares

A lender or investor who only looked at GAAP net income could dramatically underestimate how much cash a REIT actually has to pay dividends or service debt. AFFO is what determines whether a dividend is sustainable — if a REIT is paying out more in dividends than its AFFO, it's eventually paying you with money it doesn't really have.

## Check yourself

MAA reports GAAP net income of $180M for the year. Depreciation was $130M. The company spent $25M maintaining existing properties (roof repairs, HVAC replacements). What is MAA's FFO, and what is its AFFO?

> ANSWER: FFO = $180M + $130M = $310M (you add back depreciation because it's a non-cash accounting charge, not real money out the door); AFFO = $310M − $25M = $285M (you subtract the real maintenance cash spent, since that money actually left the business).

## Review

**Absorption:** A new office building opened in Dallas with 200,000 square feet of space. By end of the quarter, tenants had leased and moved into 120,000 SF — but two existing tenants elsewhere in the market each gave back 30,000 SF of space they used to occupy. What is the net absorption for the market that quarter, and what does the sign of that number tell you?

> ANSWER: Net absorption = space newly occupied minus space vacated = 120,000 − 60,000 = 60,000 SF positive; positive net absorption means the market is absorbing more space than it's giving back, a sign of healthy demand — negative net absorption would signal tenants are shrinking or leaving faster than new ones arrive.

**Triple net vs. gross leases:** A landlord has two tenants in the same building. Tenant A has a gross lease (the landlord-pays-all structure) paying $5,000/month; Tenant B has a triple net lease — NNN, meaning the tenant pays rent plus property taxes, insurance, and maintenance — paying $3,500/month base. If property taxes, insurance, and maintenance for Tenant B's space run $1,200/month, which lease is actually more valuable to the landlord, and why?

> ANSWER: Tenant B's NNN lease is more valuable — the landlord nets the full $3,500 base with no operating expense burden, while Tenant A's $5,000 gross rent requires the landlord to cover all expenses, so the landlord's actual keep depends on how high those costs are; if expenses on A's space are above $1,500/month the NNN deal wins on net cash.
