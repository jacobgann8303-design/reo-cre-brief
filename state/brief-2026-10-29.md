## Today's concept: NOI

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you rent out a room to a friend for $800 a month. But keeping that room available costs you something — utilities for that room run $50, and you pay $30 a month for a cleaning service. Your actual take-home from that room isn't $800. It's $800 minus $50 minus $30, which is **$720**. That $720 is what you actually earned from running that room. The gross rent is just the headline number; what matters is what's left after you pay to keep the lights on.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, that same idea has a name: **Net Operating Income**, or NOI. It's the money a property produces after paying its operating expenses — things like property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and management fees — but *before* paying the mortgage.

Here's a real-number example tied to today. The 10-year Treasury is at **4.99%**. Say buyers in the current market demand a risk premium (extra return for taking on the risk of owning property instead of just buying a Treasury bond) of 2% on top of that. That implies a cap rate — the ratio of NOI to property value that buyers will accept — of about 7%.

Cap rate = NOI ÷ Property Value, rearranged: Property Value = NOI ÷ Cap Rate.

If a small apartment building produces **$140,000 in gross rent** and has **$42,000 in operating expenses** (taxes, insurance, maintenance):

**NOI = $140,000 − $42,000 = $98,000**

At a 7% cap rate: $98,000 ÷ 0.07 = **$1,400,000** — that's what a buyer would pay.

### 3. Why a professional cares

NOI is the number that determines a building's value. If expenses go up — say, insurance spikes or a big repair hits — NOI falls, and so does what anyone will pay for the property. A professional underwriting (analyzing before deciding whether to buy or lend) a deal watches NOI the way you watch your bank balance: it's the real number, not the headline.

## Check yourself

A small office building collects **$200,000 in rent** per year. The owner pays **$30,000 in property taxes**, **$15,000 in insurance**, and **$25,000 in maintenance**. The mortgage payment is **$60,000 per year**. What is the NOI?

> ANSWER: NOI = $200,000 − $30,000 − $15,000 − $25,000 = **$130,000**. The mortgage payment is excluded — NOI is calculated before debt service (loan payments), so the $60,000 does not get subtracted.

## Review

**Offering memorandum (Brief #19):** A broker puts together a document to market a property for sale. What's one important thing that document is *not* designed to do for you as a buyer, and why should you read it skeptically?

> ANSWER: An offering memorandum (OM) is written by the seller's broker to make the property look as attractive as possible — it is a marketing document, not an independent analysis. The income projections and expense estimates in an OM tend to be optimistic, so a buyer should verify every number independently rather than accepting them at face value.

**Comparable sales / comps (Brief #40):** You're trying to figure out what a 20,000-square-foot retail strip center in Lubbock is worth. You find a recent sale of a 15,000-square-foot strip center in Lubbock. Is that a usable comp, and what's one adjustment you'd need to make?

> ANSWER: Yes, it's a usable starting point because it's the same property type in the same market, but you'd need to adjust for size — the sold building is 25% smaller, and price per square foot often shifts with size — so you can't apply its price per square foot directly without accounting for that difference.
