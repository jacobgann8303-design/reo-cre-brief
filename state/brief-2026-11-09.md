## Today's concept: Class A / B / C

**1. The everyday version.**
Think about apartments near campus. Some are brand-new builds with a gym, granite counters, and in-unit laundry — they charge $1,800/month. Some are solid older buildings, clean and functional but dated — maybe $1,100. Some are the ones your friends warn you about: aging, minimum maintenance, $650. Same basic product (a place to sleep), three very different tiers. That's the whole idea.

**2. The real version.**
Commercial real estate uses the same three-tier system — Class A, Class B, Class C — for every property type: office, industrial, retail, apartments. The labels aren't defined by an exact formula; they're a shorthand for location, age, condition, and what tenants you can attract.

Look at today's headlines side by side. Oppenheimer just signed a 220,000-square-foot lease at 32 Old Slip in Lower Manhattan — that's a Class A building, purpose-built or heavily renovated, in a premier location, commanding top rents. Meanwhile, delinquent DFW office loans hit $430 million. Those struggling loans are almost entirely on Class B and C buildings — the ones that lost tenants to newer Class A product and couldn't backfill them.

Here's why the math matters. If a Class A office building in Dallas trades at a 6.5% cap rate (cap rate = the building's annual income divided by its purchase price — a measure of return), and a Class B trades at 8.5%, that gap is not random. Investors demand more return to take on more risk. A $168,000 annual income at 6.5% implies a value of $168,000 ÷ 0.065 = **$2,584,615**. At 8.5% it's $168,000 ÷ 0.085 = **$1,976,471**. Same income, $608,000 difference in value — just because of the class.

**3. Why a professional cares.**
When interest rates rise — and the 10-year at 4.86% is high by recent history — Class B and C assets feel it hardest, because their cap rates were already wide and their tenant base is shakier. A broker, lender, or investor needs to know which class they're dealing with before they touch a number.

## Check yourself

A Class B office building in Fort Worth generates $200,000 per year in NOI (net operating income — the rent collected minus operating expenses, before debt payments). Investors in this market are demanding an 8.0% cap rate for Class B. A newly renovated Class A building next door generates the same $200,000 NOI but trades at a 6.0% cap rate. What is the value difference between the two buildings, and why would a lender care which one they're financing?

> ANSWER: Class B value = $200,000 ÷ 0.08 = $2,500,000; Class A value = $200,000 ÷ 0.06 = $3,333,333 — a $833,333 difference on identical income. A lender cares because the loan amount is based on property value, so they're lending against very different collateral even though the cash flow looks the same on paper.

## Review

**ARGUS (from brief #46):** You're interviewing for an analyst role at a DFW investment firm and the job posting says "ARGUS experience preferred." Without looking it up — what is ARGUS, and what specific task would you actually use it for on the job?

> ANSWER: ARGUS is financial modeling software built specifically for commercial real estate — it lets you project a property's cash flows lease-by-lease over many years, accounting for rent bumps, lease expirations, vacancy periods, and renewal assumptions; analysts use it to build the pro forma (the forward-looking income projection) that investors rely on to decide what a property is worth.

**Tenant improvement allowance and leasing commissions (from brief #22):** A landlord just signed a new tenant to a five-year office lease at $30/SF/year on 5,000 SF. Before collecting a dollar of rent, the landlord had to pay a $75,000 tenant improvement allowance (TI) and a $45,000 leasing commission (LC). What do those two costs represent, and roughly how long does it take the landlord to earn them back from rent?

> ANSWER: The TI is cash the landlord pays to build out or renovate the space to the tenant's specifications; the LC is the fee paid to the brokers who negotiated the deal — together $120,000 in upfront costs. Annual rent is $30 × 5,000 = $150,000, so the landlord needs roughly 10 months of rent ($120,000 ÷ $150,000 × 12) just to recover those costs before seeing a true profit.
