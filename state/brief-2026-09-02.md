## Today's concept: Absorption

### 1. The everyday version

Picture a new coffee shop that seats 40 people. On opening week, 10 seats fill up with regulars. The next month, 15 more people start coming in daily. The market is slowly "absorbing" the capacity that was available — seats that were empty are getting used up. If a second coffee shop opens next door and adds 40 more seats while only 10 new customers show up total, the market is struggling to absorb the new supply.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, **absorption** measures how much space the market actually took up — moved into and started paying rent on — over a given period. Specifically, **net absorption** is the change in occupied space: if 200,000 square feet were leased and 50,000 square feet were vacated, net absorption is **+150,000 square feet**.

Here's why that number matters right now: Ares and Scion just paid **$435 million for 2,316 student-housing beds**. That's roughly **$435,000,000 ÷ 2,316 beds = $187,800 per bed**. They're betting that those beds stay absorbed — occupied, paying rent — in Sun Belt markets. If absorption weakens (more beds sitting empty), that bet gets expensive fast. Meanwhile, the $10.7 billion in office loans facing maturity by 2029 is partly a story about negative absorption in office — tenants leaving, space going dark, buildings losing income.

### 3. Why a professional cares

A developer looking at a new project will check whether the local market is absorbing existing supply before adding more — if absorption is slow, new construction just makes vacancy worse and rents fall. A lender will do the same check before handing over construction financing, because a half-empty building can't repay a loan.

## Check yourself

A Sun Belt city has 1,000,000 square feet of Class A office space (Class A means the newest, highest-quality buildings in a market). Over the past year, tenants leased 120,000 square feet of new space, but companies that downsized gave back 80,000 square feet. New construction also delivered 200,000 square feet of brand-new buildings to the market. What is net absorption, and did supply outpace demand or did demand keep up?

> ANSWER: Net absorption is leased space minus vacated space: 120,000 − 80,000 = +40,000 square feet. But 200,000 square feet of new supply entered the market, far exceeding the 40,000 square feet of net demand absorbed, so vacancy almost certainly rose — supply outpaced demand by 160,000 square feet.

## Review

**Basis point (Brief #6):** The 10-year Treasury moved from 4.73% to 4.75% between yesterday and today. Without using the words "percent" or "percentage points," how large was that move, and why do professionals use that unit instead of just saying "it went up 0.02 percent"?

> ANSWER: The move was 2 basis points (each basis point equals one one-hundredth of one percent, so 0.02% = 2 bps). Professionals use basis points because small rate moves have large dollar consequences on big loans — saying "2 bps" is precise and unambiguous, while "0.02 percent" is easy to misread or confuse with a 2 percent move.

**Price per square foot (Brief #3):** A 21,500-square-foot industrial building in North Houston sold today. If it sold for $2,150,000, what is the price per square foot, and what is one thing that number alone cannot tell you about whether the deal was a good price?

> ANSWER: $2,150,000 ÷ 21,500 = $100 per square foot. That number alone can't tell you whether $100/sf is fair without knowing what comparable buildings in North Houston have sold for — the price per square foot is only useful when you compare it to similar properties in the same market.
