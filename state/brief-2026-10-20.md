## Today's concept: Replacement cost

**1. The everyday version.**

Say your car gets totaled. Your insurance company doesn't ask what you paid for it in 2019 — they ask what it would cost to buy that same car, new, today. If that number is $28,000, they're not going to pay $35,000 for your car just because someone offers you that. The replacement cost is the ceiling on what a rational person would spend.

**2. The real version.**

The same logic applies to commercial buildings. Before paying the asking price for an existing property, a buyer asks: "What would it cost me to just build this thing from scratch on an empty lot?" That figure — land plus hard construction costs (materials, labor) plus soft costs (architects, permits, financing during construction) — is the **replacement cost**.

Here's why it's urgent today. JLL says construction costs are rising 3%–6% per year. If it costs **$300 per square foot** to build a 100,000-square-foot warehouse right now, the replacement cost is:

100,000 sq ft × $300 = **$30,000,000**

If you can buy that same warehouse for $24 million because the owner needs out, you're buying at **80 cents on the replacement dollar** — a discount to replacement cost. At 6% annual construction inflation, that $30M replacement cost becomes roughly $31.8M next year. Your discount just got bigger without you doing anything.

**3. Why a professional cares.**

If a property is trading *above* replacement cost, a developer will skip buying it and just build new — which adds supply and pushes prices back down. If it's trading *below* replacement cost, new construction makes no economic sense, which protects existing owners from competition. That spread — the gap between what properties sell for and what they'd cost to build — tells you which direction the market is likely to move.

## Check yourself

A 50,000-square-foot office building is for sale at $8.5 million. You estimate it would cost $200 per square foot to build an equivalent building today, on land you could buy for $1 million. Should the replacement cost make you more or less willing to pay the asking price, and roughly by how much are you above or below it?

> ANSWER: Replacement cost = $1,000,000 (land) + (50,000 × $200) = $1,000,000 + $10,000,000 = $11,000,000. The asking price of $8.5 million is about $2.5 million *below* replacement cost, which is a signal in the buyer's favor — you'd be paying less than it would cost to build new, so the purchase is more attractive, not less.

## Review

**Sale-leaseback (brief #34):** A company owns its building, sells it to an investor, and then immediately signs a long-term lease to stay in that same building as a tenant. In one sentence: what does the company gain, and what does it give up?

> ANSWER: The company gains a lump sum of cash (it can redeploy into its core business) and gives up ownership of the building — it now pays rent instead of owning the asset free and clear.

---

**IRR vs. equity multiple (brief #15):** An investor puts $100,000 into a deal, gets back $200,000 total, but it takes 15 years. Another investor puts in $100,000 and gets back $180,000 in 3 years. The first deal has a higher equity multiple (2.0x vs. 1.8x) — but which one almost certainly has a higher IRR (Internal Rate of Return, the annualized return on invested capital), and why does that matter?

> ANSWER: The second deal almost certainly has the higher IRR because IRR measures how fast the money compounds — $180,000 back in 3 years means your dollars were working hard for a short time, while $200,000 back in 15 years means your money was tied up much longer for only a slightly bigger payoff; a higher IRR matters because you could theoretically reinvest the returned capital sooner and compound it again.
