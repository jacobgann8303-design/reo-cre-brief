## Today's concept: Tenant improvement allowance and leasing commissions

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you're renting an empty apartment, and the landlord says: "I'll give you $2,000 to fix it up how you like — paint, shelving, whatever." That $2,000 comes out of the landlord's pocket upfront, before you pay a single month of rent. The landlord is spending money now to get a tenant locked in for two years. That's the whole idea.

Now imagine the landlord also paid a friend $500 for referring you as a tenant. Same deal — money out the door before the lease even starts.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, those two costs have formal names. The **tenant improvement allowance** — pros say "TI allowance" or just "TI" — is money the landlord gives the tenant to build out the space: walls, flooring, lighting, conference rooms, whatever makes a raw shell usable for that specific business. The **leasing commission** — often called "LC" — is the fee paid to the brokers who brought the deal together, typically split between the landlord's broker and the tenant's broker.

Both are paid upfront. Both come out of the landlord's pocket before the rent clock meaningfully starts.

Here's what that looks like with real numbers. Say a landlord signs a tenant to a **5-year lease** on **5,000 square feet** at **$30/SF/year** in annual rent. The landlord offers a **$50/SF TI allowance** and pays a **leasing commission of 5%** of total lease value.

Work it out:

- Total lease value: 5,000 SF × $30 × 5 years = **$750,000**
- TI allowance: 5,000 SF × $50 = **$250,000**
- Leasing commission: $750,000 × 5% = **$37,500**
- **Total upfront cost to the landlord: $287,500** — before collecting dollar one of rent

That's real money sitting on the table before the tenant opens for business. And with the 10-year Treasury at **4.91%** right now, that $287,500 has a real cost — if the landlord borrowed it, the interest isn't cheap. If they used their own cash, they gave up a 4.91% risk-free return they could have earned elsewhere.

### 3. Why a professional cares

These two numbers — TI and LC — are the reason a lease that *looks* profitable on paper can destroy value in year one. A broker or analyst has to subtract them from the income model to find the true **effective rent** (what the landlord actually nets after handing cash back to the tenant and paying the brokers). In a market where activity just fell 14% and rates are rising, landlords are being pushed to offer *higher* TI packages to win tenants — which means the upfront hole gets deeper even as borrowing costs go up.

## Check yourself

A landlord signs a 3-year lease on 2,000 SF at $25/SF/year. She offers a $40/SF TI allowance and pays a 6% leasing commission on total lease value. What is her total upfront cost before she collects any rent — and roughly how does that compare to what she'll collect in year one?

> ANSWER: Total lease value = 2,000 × $25 × 3 = $150,000. TI = 2,000 × $40 = $80,000. Leasing commission = $150,000 × 6% = $9,000. Total upfront cost = $89,000. Year-one rent = 2,000 × $25 = $50,000. So she spends nearly $89,000 before collecting anything, and her entire first year of rent doesn't even cover it — she doesn't break even on those costs until well into year two.

## Review

**Leasable vs. rentable vs. usable square feet (Brief #56):** You're touring an office building and the landlord quotes you 1,000 rentable square feet — but when you walk the space, it only feels like 850 square feet of room you can actually put desks in. What's hiding in that gap, and what do professionals call the multiplier that creates it?

> ANSWER: The gap is the tenant's share of common areas — lobbies, hallways, restrooms, elevator banks — that the building charges rent on even though the tenant doesn't exclusively use them. The multiplier is called the **load factor** (sometimes the "add-on factor"), and it's applied to the usable square feet (the space the tenant actually occupies) to get to rentable square feet (what shows up on the lease and the rent bill).

**The CRE career ladder (Brief #45):** If you join a CRE firm straight out of undergrad, what's the first title you'd likely hold, and what does most of your day actually look like at that level?

> ANSWER: You'd almost certainly start as an **analyst** — the entry-level role — and your day is dominated by financial modeling, pulling comps (comparable property data), building spreadsheets, and running the numbers that the people above you use to make decisions; you're the engine room, not yet the person in client meetings or structuring deals.
