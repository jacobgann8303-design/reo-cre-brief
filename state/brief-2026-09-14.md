## Today's concept: Cash-on-cash return

### 1. The everyday version

Say you put $1,000 of your own money into a vending machine business. At the end of the year, after paying for restocking and the space rental, the machine puts $80 in your pocket. Your cash-on-cash return is $80 ÷ $1,000 = **8%**. That's it — how much actual cash came back to you this year, divided by how much cash you put in. It doesn't care about what the machine might be worth someday. Just: what did I get back in my hand this year?

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, you almost never pay for a building entirely with your own money. You borrow most of it. So "how much cash did you put in" means your **equity** — the portion you contributed, not the loan amount.

Here's a simplified example anchored to today's market. Suppose you're buying a small apartment complex. The purchase price is **$2,000,000**. You borrow **$1,400,000** from a bank (that's the **mortgage** — the loan secured by the property). You put in **$600,000** of your own cash. That $600,000 is your equity.

The building collects $160,000 in rent per year. After paying operating expenses (maintenance, insurance, property management — everything except the loan), you're left with **$120,000 NOI** (Net Operating Income — rent collected minus operating costs). Then you pay the bank. At today's rates, a loan around 4.95% on $1.4M might cost roughly **$84,000 per year** in debt service (the actual loan payment, covering both interest and principal repayment). That leaves:

$120,000 − $84,000 = **$36,000 cash left in your pocket**

Cash-on-cash return: $36,000 ÷ $600,000 = **6.0%**

Notice: if the 10-year Treasury is at 4.95%, your lender's rate will be higher than that — maybe 6.5–7% — which would push your debt service up and your cash-on-cash return *down*. That's exactly why today's headline rate matters to every deal being underwritten right now.

### 3. Why a professional cares

Cash-on-cash return answers the question a real investor actually lives with: "While I'm waiting to sell this thing someday, what is my money doing for me each year?" A deal might look great on paper over ten years (that's IRR — Internal Rate of Return, which we covered last week) but if the cash-on-cash return in year one is 1%, you may not be able to pay your own bills. Lenders and equity partners both use it as a quick screen: is this deal feeding its investors while it runs, or is it starving them until exit?

## Check yourself

You have $500,000 of your own cash invested in a retail strip center. After all operating expenses, the building produces $90,000 NOI per year. Your annual loan payment (debt service) is $55,000. What is your cash-on-cash return? And if the 10-year Treasury rises another 50 basis points and your lender raises your rate — pushing annual debt service up to $63,000 — what happens to that number?

> ANSWER: Cash flow after debt service = $90,000 − $55,000 = $35,000; cash-on-cash = $35,000 ÷ $500,000 = 7.0%. If debt service rises to $63,000, cash flow drops to $27,000 and cash-on-cash falls to 5.4% — showing directly how rising rates compress the return a real equity investor actually receives each year.

## Review

**Equity waterfall and promote** (from brief #14): A deal returns $3M to investors after a sale. The original equity invested was $2M, so there's $1M in profit. How does the concept of a "promote" determine whether the sponsor (the person who found and managed the deal) gets a straight 20% of that $1M, or something different — and what does the waterfall have to do with it?

> ANSWER: A waterfall sets the order in which profits are distributed — typically, investors get their money back first, then a preferred return (a promised minimum annual return, like 8%), and only after those hurdles are cleared does the promote kick in; the promote is the sponsor's disproportionate share of profits above those hurdles (often 20%), rewarding them for performance rather than giving them a flat split from dollar one.

**Debt service coverage ratio** (from brief #11): A lender is looking at a building with $180,000 NOI per year. The proposed annual loan payment would be $150,000. Will most lenders approve this loan, and why or why not?

> ANSWER: DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio) = NOI ÷ annual debt service = $180,000 ÷ $150,000 = 1.20x; most lenders require at least 1.20x to 1.25x, so this deal is right at the edge — many lenders would reject it or require a smaller loan because there is almost no cushion if income dips even slightly.
