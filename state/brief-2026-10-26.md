## Today's concept: The CRE career ladder

### 1. The everyday version

Think about a restaurant kitchen. On day one you're washing dishes. You don't touch food, but you see everything — how fast plates come back, what gets wasted, how the cooks talk to each other. After a year you move to prep cook. Then line cook. Then sous chef. Each step, you're doing more of the actual decision-making, and your mistakes cost more. Nobody skips dishwasher to head chef — the early rungs exist because judgment takes time to build.

### 2. The real version

CRE works the same way. Here's the typical path at a brokerage or investment firm:

**Analyst → Associate → Vice President (or Senior Associate) → Director / Principal → Managing Director or Partner**

At the **analyst** level — where you'd likely start — you're building spreadsheets, pulling comparable sales data (recent prices for similar properties), and formatting pitch books (presentation decks). You don't talk to clients much. You're learning the ingredients.

Here's how today's data connects. An analyst at a firm right now would be asked: *"The 10-year just dropped 17 bps to 4.96%. Update the model."* That means going into a spreadsheet and adjusting the assumed loan rate — because CRE loans are often priced as the 10-year Treasury plus a spread (an added percentage, like a markup). If the spread is 175 bps (1.75%), the loan rate just moved from roughly 5.13% + 1.75% = **6.88%** down to 4.96% + 1.75% = **6.71%**. That 0.17% difference on a $10 million loan is $17,000 per year in interest — real money that changes whether a deal pencils out (whether the numbers work).

At the **VP level**, you're deciding whether to bring that deal to the investment committee. At the **principal or partner level**, you're deciding whether to write the check.

### 3. Why a professional cares

Knowing where you are on the ladder tells you what skill to build *right now*. Analysts who understand the math get promoted faster than analysts who just execute instructions — because when rates move 17 bps at 7 a.m., the VP wants to know what it means for their live deals before 9 a.m.

On a $5,000,000 loan, how much does the annual interest payment change, and does that make the deal better or worse for the buyer?

The difference is 0.17%, and 0.17% × $5,000,000 = $8,500 less in annual interest — which makes the deal slightly better for the buyer, since debt service (the loan payment) is lower, leaving more cash flow after paying the lender.

## Review

**Going-in vs. exit cap rate (Brief #17):** A cap rate (capitalization rate) is NOI — net operating income, meaning rent collected minus operating expenses — divided by the purchase price. The *going-in* cap rate is calculated using the income the property produces on the day you buy it; the *exit* cap rate is your assumption about what a buyer will pay when you sell, years later. Here's the question: If you buy a building at a 6.0% going-in cap rate and your underwriting assumes you'll sell at a 5.5% exit cap rate — meaning future buyers will accept a smaller return — what are you betting on, and is that a conservative or aggressive assumption?

> ANSWER: You're betting that the property will be *more* valuable relative to its income at the time of sale — buyers accepting a lower return (yield compression) drives the price up, which benefits you as the seller. This is an aggressive assumption because it requires the market to improve; if cap rates stay flat or rise, your projected sale price will be lower than modeled, and your return suffers.

---

**Pro forma (Brief #18):** A pro forma is a projected financial statement — a forecast of what a property's income and expenses will be, usually over a 5- or 10-year hold period. They are built by the seller or the seller's broker and are almost always optimistic. Here's the question: A seller's pro forma shows 95% occupancy (the share of rentable space that's actually leased and paying rent) every single year of a 10-year projection. The building is currently 82% occupied. What's the right instinct when you see that, and what would you do about it?

> ANSWER: The right instinct is skepticism — jumping from 82% to 95% and holding it there for a decade is an aggressive assumption, not a conservative one; a savvy buyer would re-run the model using a more realistic lease-up timeline (the period it actually takes to fill vacant space) and lower stabilized occupancy, which would reduce projected NOI and justify a lower offer price.
