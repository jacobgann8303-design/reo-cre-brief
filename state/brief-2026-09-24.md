## Today's concept: Escalations and CPI bumps

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you sign a two-year deal with a roommate: they pay $600 a month for rent. But when you wrote the agreement, you both agreed it goes up 3% each year automatically. Year two, they owe $618 — you didn't renegotiate, the number just stepped up on a schedule you both agreed to at the start. That automatic increase baked into a long-term agreement is called an **escalation**.

Some escalations aren't a fixed percentage — they're tied to a measuring stick called the **Consumer Price Index, or CPI**. The CPI (published by the U.S. government) tracks the average price of everyday goods and services — groceries, gas, rent, healthcare. When CPI goes up 4%, inflation went up 4%. An escalation clause tied to CPI means: "your payment rises by whatever inflation rose."

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, almost every long-term lease has escalation clauses built in. A tenant signs a 10-year office lease at $40 per square foot per year. The lease says rent increases **3% per year**, fixed. Here's what happens:

- Year 1: $40.00/sf
- Year 2: $40.00 × 1.03 = **$41.20/sf**
- Year 3: $41.20 × 1.03 = **$42.44/sf**
- Year 5: roughly **$46.37/sf**

Over 10 years that lease generates meaningfully more income than $40 × 10 would suggest — and that growing income stream is part of what a buyer pays for.

Now tie it to today: the 10-year Treasury is at **4.96%**. If you're a landlord with a lease that only escalates 2% per year, you're collecting rent that grows slower than the risk-free return on a Treasury bond — meaning inflation, which has been running hot, may be outpacing your rent growth. That matters when underwriting — that is, when calculating whether a deal pencils out financially.

A CPI-linked escalation solves this differently: if CPI runs 4% in a given year, rent goes up 4%. If CPI drops to 1.5%, rent only goes up 1.5%. It floats with actual inflation rather than a fixed step.

### 3. Why a professional cares

When a buyer is calculating NOI — Net Operating Income, meaning the income a property produces after operating expenses — they're not just looking at today's rent. They're projecting what rents will be in years 3, 5, and 10. A lease with strong escalations makes that future income higher and more predictable, which supports a higher purchase price today. A lease with no escalations — or below-inflation escalations — means the landlord slowly loses ground to rising costs, which drags down what a buyer will pay.

## Check yourself

A tenant signs a 5-year lease at $30/sf with a fixed 2% annual escalation. A buyer is considering purchasing the building and wants to know what rent that tenant will be paying in Year 4. What is the Year 4 rent, and if CPI averages 4% per year over that same period, is the landlord keeping up with inflation?

> ANSWER: Year 4 rent = $30 × 1.02 × 1.02 × 1.02 = $30 × (1.02)³ ≈ $31.84/sf. No — the landlord is not keeping up with inflation. At 4% CPI, the landlord would need rents around $30 × (1.04)³ ≈ $33.74/sf to stay even; a 2% fixed escalation falls about $1.90/sf short, meaning the real purchasing power of that rent has declined.

## Review

**Triple net vs. gross lease (Brief #21):** You're looking at two retail leases side by side. Lease A is $25/sf gross. Lease B is $18/sf NNN (triple net). Which one actually puts more operating cost risk on the tenant, and what does that mean for how you compare the two?

> ANSWER: Lease B (NNN) puts more cost risk on the tenant — in a triple-net lease, the tenant pays base rent plus property taxes, insurance, and maintenance on top of it, so $18/sf could easily become $24–26/sf in total cost once those expenses are added. To compare them fairly you need to estimate the NNN expenses and add them to the $18 base; only then can you tell which deal is actually cheaper for the tenant or more valuable to the landlord.
