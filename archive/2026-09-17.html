Today's concept: Offering memorandum

The everyday version.

Imagine you're selling your car. Before a serious buyer comes to look at it, you put together a packet: photos, the Carfax report, the mileage, what you replaced recently, and your asking price. You're not hiding anything — but you're also putting your best foot forward. The packet is designed to make the car look as attractive as possible while still being truthful enough that you can't be sued later.

The real version.

In commercial real estate, that packet is called an offering memorandum — often shortened to OM or offering memo. When a seller hires a broker to sell a property, the broker produces an OM: a polished document with photos, a description of the property, a map of the market, tenant information, and — most importantly — a financial summary showing the income the property generates.

Here's where it gets real. That financial summary is almost always a pro forma (taught yesterday — a projection of future income, not a record of what actually happened). And the pro forma inside an OM is written to support the seller's asking price. Say a seller wants $2,400,000 for an office building. They might project Net Operating Income (NOI — the rent collected minus the costs of running the building, before debt payments) of $168,000. That gives a cap rate (the return you'd earn if you paid cash — NOI ÷ purchase price) of:

$168,000 ÷ $2,400,000 = 7.0%

With today's 10-year Treasury at 5.00%, a buyer needs to earn meaningfully more than 5% to justify the risk of owning real estate over just buying a government bond. A 7.0% cap rate looks like it clears that bar — but only if the $168,000 NOI is real. If the OM assumes rents that aren't actually in the leases yet, that 7.0% is fiction.

Why a professional cares.

An experienced buyer reads an OM the way you read an ad — interested, but skeptical. The OM gets you in the door; your own underwriting (your independent financial analysis of whether the deal actually works) tells you whether to make an offer. The single most important question to ask about any OM is: *what assumptions would have to be wrong for this deal to fail?*

Check yourself

A seller's OM shows projected NOI of $210,000 and an asking price of $3,000,000. The 10-year Treasury is at 5.00% today. A buyer does their own analysis and concludes actual, in-place NOI (income already locked in by signed leases) is only $180,000. What cap rate does the OM imply, what cap rate does the buyer's own analysis imply, and why does the gap matter?

Show answer

The OM implies a cap rate of $210,000 ÷ $3,000,000 = 7.0%, but the buyer's real number is $180,000 ÷ $3,000,000 = 6.0% — only 1 point above the risk-free 10-year Treasury rate, which is thin compensation for the risk of owning real estate; the gap tells the buyer the seller is pricing in income that doesn't exist yet, and the buyer should either negotiate the price down or walk away.

Review

Going-in vs. exit cap rate (from brief #17): When a buyer underwrites a deal — meaning they build their own financial model to test whether it works — they use two different cap rates: one at purchase and one at the assumed sale years later. Which cap rate is typically *higher*, the going-in or the exit, and why does that conservative assumption protect the buyer?

Show answer

The exit cap rate is typically set *higher* than the going-in cap rate, because a higher cap rate means a lower implied value — so if you assume the property sells at a higher cap rate than you bought it, you're building in a cushion; if the market softens and buyers demand more return, your projections already anticipated that instead of assuming the good times last forever.

Pro forma (from brief #18): A broker sends you an OM with a pro forma showing 5% annual rent growth for five years. The current average rent in the building is $28/SF, and the market average nearby is $29/SF. Does the pro forma's rent growth assumption seem aggressive, conservative, or roughly fair — and what's the quick logic?

Show answer

The assumption is likely aggressive — rents are already close to market ($28 vs. $29/SF), so there's almost no room to grow rents by catching up to market rates; to hit 5% annual growth from an already-near-market starting point, the market itself would have to keep climbing steeply every year, which is a bet, not a safe assumption.
