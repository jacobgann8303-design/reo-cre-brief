Today's concept: ARGUS

1. The everyday version

Imagine you're buying a used car that you plan to rent out to friends over the next five years. Before you pay for it, you want to project: how much rent will each friend pay, month by month? When will the engine need work? What will the car sell for at the end? You don't just add it all up in your head — you build a spreadsheet that maps out every cash flow, year by year, so you can see the total picture and decide what the car is worth *today*.

2. The real version

ARGUS (the name is a legacy acronym — what matters is what it does, not what the letters stand for) is the industry-standard software that does that same projection for a commercial building. You feed it every lease in the building — tenant name, square footage, rent per square foot, when the lease expires, what happens after it expires — and ARGUS spits out year-by-year cash flows for the life of the deal, typically ten years.

Here's why today's numbers matter. Say a Dallas office building has $1,000,000 in NOI (Net Operating Income — what's left from rent after paying operating expenses, before paying the mortgage). At a 7% cap rate (cap rate = NOI ÷ purchase price, rearranged: price = NOI ÷ cap rate), the building is worth:

$1,000,000 ÷ 0.07 = $14,285,714

But ARGUS doesn't just use today's NOI. It models what happens in year 3 when Tenant A's lease expires, in year 5 when rents might grow, and in year 10 when you sell. With the 10-year Treasury at 5.00% — the benchmark against which CRE returns are measured — investors are demanding higher returns (higher cap rates) than they were two years ago when rates were lower. ARGUS lets you stress-test: what does the deal look like if the cap rate at sale is 7.5% instead of 7.0%? You'd sell for:

$1,000,000 ÷ 0.075 = $13,333,333 — about $950,000 less.

That gap changes your decision about what to pay today.

3. Why a professional cares

Every acquisition analyst at a CRE firm runs ARGUS before recommending a deal — the output is what gets presented to the investment committee. If you can't build and read an ARGUS model, you can't work in acquisitions or asset management at a serious shop. It's the difference between having an opinion about a building and being able to prove it with numbers.

Check yourself

A friend tells you a building has an NOI of $500,000 today. You run it through ARGUS and find that one big tenant — paying $150,000 a year — has a lease that expires in year 2 with no renewal option. Your model shows that without that tenant, NOI drops to $350,000 in year 3. If buyers in that market use a 6.5% cap rate, what is the difference in value between the building *with* and *without* that tenant in place? Show your math.

Show answer

With the tenant: $500,000 ÷ 0.065 = $7,692,308. Without the tenant: $350,000 ÷ 0.065 = $5,384,615. The difference is about $2,307,693 — that lease expiration risk is worth over $2.3 million in value, which is exactly why ARGUS modeling of lease rollover (when leases expire and must be re-leased) drives acquisition pricing.

Review

CMBS question: You see a Trepp report on CMBS delinquency rates rising. In one sentence, what is CMBS — what did someone do to create it, and why does a rising delinquency rate matter to investors who own it?

Show answer

CMBS (Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities) are bonds created by bundling together many commercial real estate loans and selling slices of the bundle to investors; when delinquency rates rise — meaning more borrowers are missing payments — investors in those bonds receive less cash than expected, and the bonds lose value.

---

Preferred equity vs. mezzanine debt question: A CRE deal goes into a workout — the borrower can't make payments and lenders are deciding who gets paid first. You hold preferred equity in the deal; your colleague holds mezzanine debt (a loan that sits between the senior mortgage and the equity). In a default, who has the stronger legal position, and why does the distinction matter now?

Show answer

The mezzanine debt holder has the stronger position because debt — even junior debt — gets paid before any equity in a default; preferred equity sits in the equity layer and only gets paid after all debt is satisfied, meaning in a distressed workout, preferred equity holders often recover little or nothing while mezzanine lenders can foreclose on the equity and take control of the asset.
