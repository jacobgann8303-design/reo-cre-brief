Today's concept: Pro forma

1. The everyday version

Imagine you're deciding whether to buy a used car and flip it for a profit. Before you spend a dollar, you sit down and write out your best guess: "I think I can buy it for $8,000, spend $1,500 fixing it, and sell it for $12,000 in six months." That sheet of guesses — your expected costs, expected revenue, expected profit — is a pro forma. It's not what actually happened. It's what you *project* will happen, built before the deal closes.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, a pro forma is a projected income statement for a property — usually covering five to ten years — that an investor builds *before* buying. It lays out expected rent income, expected vacancy (empty units), expected operating expenses, and the resulting NOI (Net Operating Income — what's left after operating expenses, before debt payments).

Here's a simple example tied to today's market. Say you're looking at a small apartment building in McKinney, Texas — right where VanTrust just broke ground, so the area is active. You project:

  • Gross rent income: $300,000/year
  • Vacancy (assume 5% of units empty on average): −$15,000
  • Operating expenses (maintenance, insurance, management): −$117,000
  • Projected NOI: $168,000

You're being asked to pay $2,400,000 for the building.

Implied cap rate (NOI ÷ purchase price — the annual return if you paid all cash):

$168,000 ÷ $2,400,000 = 7.0%

With the 10-year Treasury at 4.97% today, that 7.0% cap rate gives you about 203 basis points of spread (extra return above the risk-free rate). Whether that's enough cushion is exactly what the pro forma helps you argue.

3. Why a professional cares

A pro forma is the document every lender, equity partner, and buyer argues over — change one assumption (say, rent growth drops from 3% to 1% per year) and the projected returns can swing enough to kill a deal. The pro forma is also where deals get dressed up or quietly broken: optimistic rent assumptions are called "pro forma rents," and experienced investors always ask what the *in-place* rents (what tenants actually pay today) are versus what the pro forma *projects* they'll be.

Check yourself

Question: Your pro forma projects NOI of $210,000 in Year 1 on a building you're buying for $3,000,000. A lender looks at the deal and says your vacancy assumption is too low — they want you to underwrite 8% vacancy instead of your 3%. Your gross rent is $250,000. What does that do to your projected NOI, and how does it change the implied cap rate?

Show answer

At 3% vacancy you lose $7,500, leaving $242,500 gross before expenses; at 8% vacancy you lose $20,000, leaving $230,000 — a difference of $12,500 in gross income. If operating expenses stay fixed, NOI drops by $12,500 (from $210,000 to $197,500). The implied cap rate falls from $210,000 ÷ $3,000,000 = 7.0% to $197,500 ÷ $3,000,000 = 6.58%, which makes the deal look less attractive and may affect how much a lender is willing to loan.

Review

Cash-on-cash return (taught in brief #16): You put $500,000 of your own cash into a deal as a down payment. After paying the mortgage and all expenses, the property sends you $35,000 in cash distributions this year. What is your cash-on-cash return, and what does that number actually measure — what does it ignore?

Show answer

Cash-on-cash return = $35,000 ÷ $500,000 = 7.0%. It measures the cash you actually receive in your pocket this year as a percentage of what you put in — but it ignores appreciation (the property gaining value over time) and principal paydown on the loan, so it's a snapshot of current income only, not total return.

The capital stack (taught in brief #13): A deal is funded with $1,000,000 in senior debt (a bank loan — the first to get paid back, lowest risk), $200,000 in mezzanine debt (a second loan that sits behind the bank, higher risk), and $300,000 in common equity (the owner's cash, last to get paid, highest risk). If the property sells for only $900,000, who gets hurt and why?

Show answer

The senior debt lender gets paid first and is owed $1,000,000 — but the sale only brings $900,000, so the senior lender takes a $100,000 loss, the mezzanine lender gets nothing, and the equity investor gets nothing; the capital stack means losses flow upward from the bottom (equity) first, but in this case the loss is large enough to reach even the senior lender.
