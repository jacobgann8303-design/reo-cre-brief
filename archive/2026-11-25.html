Today's concept: Triple net (NNN) vs. gross vs. modified gross

1. The everyday version

Imagine you rent an apartment. In one deal, your landlord covers everything — water, trash, building insurance, property taxes — and you just write one check for $1,200 a month. In another deal, you pay $900 a month, but you also pay your own utilities, renter's insurance, and a share of the building's water bill. Same apartment. Very different total cost. The question of who pays the operating expenses is the whole game.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, a gross lease is like the first apartment: the tenant pays one flat number and the landlord handles all the building's operating expenses — property taxes, insurance, and maintenance. A triple net (NNN) lease is like the second: the tenant pays a lower base rent plus their share of property taxes, building insurance, and maintenance costs (those three are the "three nets"). A modified gross lease splits it somewhere in the middle — maybe the landlord covers taxes and insurance, but the tenant pays their own utilities.

Here's why the math matters. Suppose a retail building has a gross rent of $25 per square foot and those three expense categories total $8 per square foot per year. Under a gross lease, the landlord collects $25 but nets only $25 − $8 = $17 after expenses. Under a NNN lease, the landlord collects $17 as base rent and passes the $8 directly to the tenant — so the landlord still nets $17, but the tenant's all-in cost is still $25. The income to the landlord is the same; the exposure to rising expenses is not.

Now tie it to today: the 10-year Treasury is at 4.92%. Property insurance and maintenance costs have been climbing. Under a gross lease, every dollar of expense increase eats directly into the landlord's NOI (Net Operating Income — the income left after operating expenses, before debt payments). Under a NNN lease, those increases pass straight through to the tenant. With rates and costs both elevated right now, landlords writing new leases strongly prefer NNN structure.

3. Why a professional cares

When a broker or investor underwrites a deal (analyzes it to decide what it's worth), they need to know the lease structure before they can trust any income number. A building showing $500,000 of "rent income" under a gross lease is worth less than the same $500,000 under NNN — because under gross, the landlord is still on the hook for expenses that could easily run $100,000. Same headline number, different actual value.

Check yourself

A landlord owns a 10,000-square-foot retail building. A tenant offers to sign either (A) a gross lease at $30/SF/year, or (B) a NNN lease at $22/SF/year. The landlord estimates taxes, insurance, and maintenance will run $7/SF/year. Which lease puts more net income in the landlord's pocket, and by how much per year?

Show answer

Under the gross lease, the landlord collects $30 × 10,000 = $300,000 but pays $7 × 10,000 = $70,000 in expenses, netting $230,000. Under the NNN lease, the landlord collects $22 × 10,000 = $220,000 and passes all expenses to the tenant, netting $220,000. The gross lease nets $10,000 more per year — but if expenses rise above $8/SF, the NNN lease becomes the better deal for the landlord, because the tenant absorbs the increase.

Review

CAM reconciliation (from brief #28): At the end of a lease year, a landlord sits down and compares what a tenant actually paid toward common area maintenance (the shared costs of running a building — lobbies, parking lots, HVAC, cleaning) against what the building actually spent. What is this process called, and what happens if the tenant underpaid?

Show answer

This is called a CAM reconciliation (Common Area Maintenance reconciliation) — the annual true-up between estimated CAM charges collected monthly and actual expenses incurred; if the tenant underpaid, they owe the landlord a catch-up payment, and if they overpaid, the landlord issues a credit or refund.

Broker vs. principal vs. advisor (from brief #44): Three people are in a room for a building sale: one person owns the building and is selling it, one person is paid a commission to represent that seller and find a buyer, and one person is a pension fund consultant advising the buyer on whether the price is fair. Which one is the principal, which is the broker, and which is the advisor?

Show answer

The owner selling the building is the principal — they have their own money at stake in the transaction; the person earning a commission to find a buyer is the broker — they facilitate the deal for a fee but don't own the asset; the pension fund consultant giving independent guidance is the advisor — they are paid for their opinion, not for closing the deal.
