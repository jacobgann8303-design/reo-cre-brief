Today's concept: Class A / B / C

1. The everyday version.

Think about apartments in Lubbock. There's a brand-new complex with a pool, stainless appliances, and a gym — charges $1,400 a month. A few miles away, a solid 1990s complex, clean but nothing fancy — charges $950. Then there's an older building from the 1970s, functional, basic, $650. Same product (a place to live), three completely different tiers. The building, the finishes, the location, and the age all determine which bucket it falls into.

2. The real version.

Commercial real estate uses the same three-tier system, called Class A, Class B, and Class C. Class A is the newest, best-located, highest-quality building in a market — it commands the highest rents and attracts the strongest tenants. Class B is a step down: older, or slightly worse location, or less updated — still functional, but not the trophy. Class C is the oldest or most worn, lowest rents, sometimes a redevelopment candidate.

Here's why it matters today. The 10-year Treasury is at 4.79%. Propmodo is reporting that cap rates must rise as Treasury yields rise. A Class A office building in Miami — the Bisnow headline notes Miami is about to get its first new towers in 15 years — might trade at a 5.5% cap rate (meaning buyers accept a smaller return because the asset is perceived as safer). A Class B building in the same city might need to offer a 7.0% cap rate to attract a buyer. If the 10-year pushes both of those cap rates up by 50 basis points, the Class B building's value drops *more in dollar terms* because its starting cap rate was already higher and buyers have less patience for risk when risk-free Treasuries pay 4.79%.

The math: a building with $1,000,000 in NOI (Net Operating Income — rent collected minus operating expenses, before debt payments) at a 5.5% cap rate is worth $1,000,000 ÷ 0.055 = $18.2 million. Push that cap rate to 6.0% and it's worth $1,000,000 ÷ 0.060 = $16.7 million. Same income, $1.5 million less value — just from a 50 basis point move in cap rate.

3. Why a professional cares.

A lender, a broker, and a buyer all use the class designation to set their risk tolerance immediately — before they ever open a spreadsheet. If someone tells you it's a Class C retail strip in a secondary market, you already know the financing will be harder, the tenant pool thinner, and the cap rate higher. The class is the shorthand that gets everyone to the same starting point fast.

Check yourself

A Class B apartment building in Denver produces $420,000 in NOI per year. Comparable Class B deals in that market are trading at a 6.0% cap rate. A buyer is considering it. Then the 10-year Treasury jumps 75 basis points and investors start demanding a 6.75% cap rate on Class B apartments instead.

What is the building worth *before* the rate move, and what is it worth *after*? How much value did the owner lose — and did the NOI change at all?

Show answer

Before: $420,000 ÷ 0.060 = $7,000,000. After: $420,000 ÷ 0.0675 = $6,222,222. The owner lost roughly $777,778 in value — the NOI didn't change at all, only what buyers were willing to pay per dollar of income changed, driven by the rise in Treasury yields making competing investments more attractive.

Review

Vacancy vs. availability (brief #7): A landlord is marketing a 10,000 sq ft office suite as available for lease, but the current tenant's lease doesn't expire for eight more months and they're still paying rent. Is that space vacant? Is it available? What's the difference between the two numbers, and which one is larger in a typical market?

Show answer

The space is available (the landlord is actively marketing it) but not yet vacant (a tenant still occupies and pays for it); availability includes both empty spaces and spaces being marketed before current leases expire, so the availability rate is almost always higher than the vacancy rate in a given market.

The four asset classes (brief #4): Name the four traditional commercial real estate asset classes, and explain in one sentence why data centers are now sometimes treated as a fifth — rather than just being filed under "industrial."

Show answer

The four traditional classes are office, industrial, retail, and multifamily; data centers are increasingly treated separately because their tenants, lease structures, power requirements, and investor base are so specialized that lumping them with standard warehouses obscures more than it reveals.
