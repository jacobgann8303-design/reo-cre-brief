Today's concept: Equity waterfall and promote

1. The everyday version

Imagine you and a friend flip a house together. You put up all the cash — $80,000 — because your friend found the deal and will manage the renovation. You both agree: when you sell, you get your $80,000 back first, then an 8% annual return on it. After that, you split whatever's left — but your friend gets a bigger slice (say 30%) even though they put in no money, because they did the work and took the risk of finding it. That bonus slice your friend earns above a straight 50/50 split is called the promote (sometimes called carried interest). The whole sequence — who gets paid what, in what order — is the waterfall.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, the shape is identical. Take the Blackstone–AWS data center deal announced today: $5 billion in assets, launched at a 7% cap rate (cap rate = Net Operating Income ÷ Purchase Price; it tells you the unlevered yield on the property). Blackstone raised equity from investors — call them the limited partners (LPs), the passive money — and Blackstone itself is the general partner (GP), the one sourcing and managing the deal.

A typical waterfall looks like this:

  • Tier 1 — Return of capital: LPs get their invested dollars back first.
  • Tier 2 — Preferred return: LPs earn a preferred return (let's say 8%) on their capital before anyone else touches profits. At 7% cap rate on $5B with 60% leverage (meaning $3B in debt), the equity is roughly $2B. 8% preferred on $2B = $160M/year that flows to LPs before the GP sees a cent of upside.
  • Tier 3 — The promote: Once LPs clear their 8%, remaining profits split — say 80% to LPs, 20% to Blackstone as the GP. That extra 20% above Blackstone's actual ownership stake is the promote.

$160,000,000 ÷ $2,000,000,000 = 8.0% — that's the preferred return hurdle the deal must clear before Blackstone earns its bonus.

3. Why a professional cares

The 10-year is at 5.10% today. When the risk-free rate — the return you get from lending to the U.S. government with zero risk — is 5.10%, a GP promising LPs only an 8% preferred return is offering a spread of less than 3% over doing nothing. LPs will push that hurdle higher, or walk. That compression makes it harder to structure deals that work for everyone in the waterfall — which is exactly why transaction volume drops when rates rise.

Check yourself

A deal has $1,000,000 of LP equity in it. The waterfall says LPs get a 9% preferred return before the GP earns any promote. The property generates $80,000 of cash flow this year available to distribute.

Does the GP earn any promote this year, and how much cash does each party receive?

Show answer

No promote. The 9% preferred return requires $90,000 ($1,000,000 × 0.09) before the GP earns anything, but only $80,000 is available — so the full $80,000 goes to the LPs, the preferred return is not fully met, and the GP receives zero promote this year.

Review

Submarket (taught in brief #39): You hear a broker say "the DFW office market is softening." Why is that statement almost useless to an investor trying to decide whether to buy a specific building in Uptown Dallas?

Show answer

"DFW" is too broad to act on — it lumps together dozens of distinct submarkets (Uptown, Las Colinas, Frisco, etc.) that each have their own vacancy, rent, and demand trends; a submarket is the specific geographic pocket that actually competes for the same tenants, and Uptown could be tightening even while DFW overall softens.

The four asset classes (taught in brief #50): Name the four traditional commercial real estate asset classes. Today's headlines mention a $5 billion deal that arguably doesn't fit neatly into any of them — what is it, and why did it break the list?

Show answer

The four traditional classes are office, industrial, retail, and multifamily; the $5 billion Blackstone–AWS deal is a data center, which has grown so large and specialized — with its own demand drivers (cloud computing, AI), lease structures, and tenant base) — that most practitioners now treat it as a fifth asset class rather than forcing it into industrial.
