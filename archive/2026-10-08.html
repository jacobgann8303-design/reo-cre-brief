Today's concept: Opportunity Zones

1. The everyday version

Imagine the government wants to get money flowing into a struggling neighborhood — maybe a block in town where storefronts have been empty for years. So it offers a deal: if you take profits you made somewhere else (say, you sold a stock at a gain) and pour that money into that neighborhood instead of pocketing it, the government will let you delay and potentially reduce the taxes you owe on those profits. The longer you leave your money in the neighborhood, the better the tax deal gets. It's basically a tax incentive — a reward for choosing to invest in a place most people would otherwise skip.

2. The real version

In CRE, an Opportunity Zone is a census tract (a defined geographic area, roughly a neighborhood) designated by the federal government as economically distressed. When an investor sells an asset — say, an apartment building — and has a capital gain (profit on the sale), they normally owe capital gains tax right away. Under the Opportunity Zone program, if they roll that gain into a Qualified Opportunity Fund (a special investment vehicle that puts money into Opportunity Zone properties) within 180 days, they can:

  • Defer paying tax on the original gain until 2026 (the current deadline under the program's rules),
  • Reduce the taxable amount if they hold long enough, and
  • Eliminate any new gains on the Opportunity Zone investment itself if they hold it at least 10 years.

Here's why today's market makes this interesting. The 10-year Treasury is at 5.27%. That means a plain, safe government bond pays you 5.27% per year. For an Opportunity Zone deal to compete with that — especially in a risky, underdeveloped neighborhood — the tax savings have to be big enough to close the gap. At current rates, the math is harder than it was in 2019 when the 10-year was around 2%. The tax benefit is the same; the opportunity cost (what you give up by not buying safe bonds instead) is much higher.

Quick illustration of the elimination benefit: You invest $1,000,000 of capital gains into a Qualified Opportunity Fund. Over 10 years, the property appreciates to $1,800,000. That $800,000 of new appreciation? $0 in federal capital gains tax if you hold the full 10 years. Without the program, at a 20% capital gains rate: $800,000 × 20% = $160,000 owed to the IRS.

3. Why a professional cares

A deal sponsor (the person putting together the investment) uses the Opportunity Zone designation as a marketing tool: they can pitch investors on the tax elimination benefit to attract capital that might otherwise go to a simpler, safer investment. In a high-rate environment like today — 10-year at 5.27% — investors demand more return for any risky bet, so the tax benefit becomes a larger part of the pitch rather than a bonus on top of an already-attractive deal.

Check yourself

You have a client who realized a $500,000 capital gain selling an industrial building in June 2026. They're deciding whether to roll it into a Qualified Opportunity Fund or just pay the tax and buy 10-year Treasuries at 5.27%. What is the immediate tax cost they're deferring if the federal capital gains rate is 20%, and what do they give up in annual yield if they had instead put that after-tax amount into Treasuries?

Show answer

The immediate tax bill they defer is $500,000 × 20% = $100,000; if they paid that tax and invested the remaining $400,000 in 10-year Treasuries at 5.27%, they would earn $400,000 × 5.0027 = $21,080 per year in risk-free yield — that $21,080 annual yield is the opportunity cost they're giving up each year while the money sits in the Opportunity Zone fund, which is why higher rates make the Opportunity Zone trade-off harder to justify unless the tax elimination benefit on future appreciation is large enough to compensate.

Review

Review 1 — Leasable vs. rentable vs. usable square feet (Brief #10)

You're touring an office suite. The landlord says it's 4,000 rentable square feet, but when you walk it, it only feels like 3,400 square feet of actual workspace. Where did those 600 square feet go, and what is the ratio of rentable to usable called?

Show answer

The 600 square feet is the tenant's allocated share of common areas — lobbies, hallways, restrooms, mechanical rooms — added onto the actual usable space (3,400 SF) to get the rentable square feet (4,000 SF) that rent is charged on; the ratio of rentable to usable (4,000 ÷ 3,400 ≈ 1.176, or a 17.6% add-on) is called the load factor, sometimes called the common area factor.

Review 2 — CAM reconciliation (Brief #28)

A tenant signs a lease where they pay $2,000 per month in estimated CAM charges — CAM stands for Common Area Maintenance, the tenant's share of operating costs like landscaping, parking lot upkeep, and building insurance.
