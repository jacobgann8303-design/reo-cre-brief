Today's concept: Lease rollover risk and the maturity wall

1. The everyday version

Imagine you have three roommates and all three leases happen to expire the same month. Suddenly you need all three of them to re-sign at once — or find replacements — or you can't cover rent. If the rental market has softened and one roommate knows you're desperate, they'll negotiate hard on their share. You have no leverage because you need all three at the same time.

2. The real version

That same problem plays out in commercial real estate at two levels: the building and the loan.

Lease rollover risk is what happens when a significant chunk of a building's leases expire around the same time. If tenants don't renew — or renew at lower rents — the building's NOI (Net Operating Income — total rent collected minus operating expenses, before debt payments) drops. A lower NOI means a lower property value, because value is priced as:

> Property Value = NOI ÷ Cap Rate

Cap rate (capitalization rate — the return a buyer expects from the property's income alone, expressed as a percentage) is set by the market. If NOI falls and cap rate stays the same, value falls proportionally.

The maturity wall is the loan version of the same problem. Many commercial loans are 5- or 10-year terms with a balloon payment — meaning the full remaining balance is due at maturity, not paid off gradually. Right now, billions of dollars of loans originated when rates were near zero are maturing and must be refinanced at today's 5.18% 10-year Treasury, plus whatever spread (extra percentage points above the Treasury rate that a lender charges for the risk of a CRE loan) the lender demands. A loan that cost 3.5% in 2016 might cost 7–8% today.

Here's the arithmetic. A building generating $500,000 NOI at a 6.0% cap rate is worth:

> $500,000 ÷ 0.06 = $8,333,333

If two big tenants roll (industry shorthand for "their leases expire and they leave or pay less") and NOI falls to $375,000, the same cap rate gives:

> $375,000 ÷ 0.06 = $6,250,000

That's a $2,083,333 drop in value — and the owner still owes the original loan balance. The Centre Square CMBS story in today's headlines is that math playing out in real life.

3. Why a professional cares

A lender reviewing a loan renewal will look at the lease expiration schedule before approving anything — if 40% of leases roll in the same year the loan matures, that's a red flag that can kill the refinancing or force the owner to inject more equity (cash) to make the numbers work. An investor underwriting (evaluating before buying) a building today will price the rollover risk by projecting lower rents for expiring leases and stress-testing the value at today's refinancing rates, not the rates from when the loan was originated.

Check yourself

A 200,000 square-foot office building has an NOI of $3,000,000 and sells at a 7.0% cap rate. Two years later, the anchor tenant (the largest, most important tenant — you learned this last week) — occupying 80,000 square feet — doesn't renew, and the space sits vacant for 12 months. During that vacancy the building's NOI drops to $1,800,000. The original loan was $30,000,000. At the new NOI, what is the building worth at the same 7.0% cap rate, and is there enough value to cover the loan?

Show answer

$1,800,000 ÷ 0.07 = $25,714,286 — the building is now worth about $25.7 million, which is $4.3 million less than the $30 million loan balance, meaning the property is underwater (worth less than what's owed) and the lender is exposed to a loss if the owner sells or defaults.

Review

Review 1 — Tenant improvement allowance and leasing commissions (Brief #22):

A landlord just signed a new 5-year lease for 10,000 square feet at $30/SF per year. The landlord agreed to a $50/SF tenant improvement allowance (a cash contribution the landlord makes to help the tenant build out their space) and will pay a leasing commission (a fee paid to the broker who brought the tenant) of 5% of total lease value. What is the total upfront cash cost the landlord pays before collecting a dollar of rent, and roughly how many months of rent does it take just to recover those two costs?

Show answer

Total lease value = $30 × 10,000 × 5 = $1,500,000; leasing commission = 5% × $1,500,000 = $75,000; TI allowance = $50 × 10,000 = $500,000; total upfront cost = $575,000; annual rent = $300,000, so recovery takes $575,000 ÷ $25,000/month ≈ 23 months — nearly two years of rent just to break even on signing costs.

Review 2 — The 10-year Treasury and cap rate spread (Brief #5):

The 10-year Treasury is at 5.18% today. If investors in a given market typically demand a spread (extra return above the Treasury rate to compensate for the additional risk of owning real estate rather than a government bond) of 200 basis points on industrial properties, what cap rate would you expect buyers to require — and what does it mean for property values if the Treasury yield keeps rising?

Show answer

5.18% + 2.00% (200 basis points) = 7.18% cap rate; if the Treasury keeps rising, the required cap rate rises with it, and since Property Value = NOI ÷ Cap Rate, a higher cap rate means buyers pay less for the same NOI — rising rates directly compress property values even if the building's income doesn't change.
