Today's concept: Debt service coverage ratio

1. The everyday version

Imagine you take home $3,000 a month. Your car payment, rent, and loan payments together cost $1,500. You're covering your bills twice over — you have room to breathe. Now imagine those payments rise to $2,900. You're still technically paying everything, but one surprise expense and you're in trouble. A lender looking at you would feel very different about those two situations, even though you're making the same income in both.

2. The real version

That same logic applies to a commercial building. The number lenders use is called the Debt Service Coverage Ratio, or DSCR — pronounced "dee-es-see-ar" or sometimes "disher." Here's the formula:

DSCR = NOI ÷ Annual Debt Service

NOI (Net Operating Income — the income a property produces after operating expenses but before loan payments and taxes) is the building's take-home pay. Annual Debt Service is the total of all loan payments for the year — principal plus interest.

Let's make it real. Say you own an apartment building with an NOI of $300,000. You have a loan with annual payments of $220,000.

> $300,000 ÷ $220,000 = 1.36 DSCR

That means the building earns $1.36 for every $1.00 it owes. Most lenders require a minimum of around 1.20 to 1.25 — enough cushion that a few vacancies or a rent dip won't blow up the loan.

Now connect this to today. The 10-year Treasury is at 4.88%, which pushes loan interest rates higher. If that same building refinances at a higher rate, the annual debt service might jump from $220,000 to $280,000:

> $300,000 ÷ $280,000 = 1.07 DSCR

That's below most lenders' minimums. The loan gets denied — which is exactly what Trepp is reporting for 75% of maturing office CMBS loans right now.

3. Why a professional cares

DSCR is often the single number that determines whether a deal gets financed. If it falls below the lender's floor, the borrower must either bring more cash to pay down the loan, raise rents, or sell — there's no workaround.

Check yourself

A small industrial building has an NOI of $180,000 per year. A lender offers a loan with annual payments of $160,000. The lender requires a minimum DSCR of 1.25.

Does this deal get financed? If not, what would the NOI need to be for it to just barely qualify?

Show answer

$180,000 ÷ $160,000 = 1.125 DSCR, which is below the 1.25 minimum, so the lender declines. To just barely qualify, NOI would need to be 1.25 × $160,000 = $200,000 — the building is $20,000 of annual income short.

Review

Cap rate (brief #47): You're looking at two apartment buildings. Building A sold at a 5% cap rate and Building B sold at an 8% cap rate — meaning buyers paid different prices relative to the income each building produces. Which one did buyers pay more for per dollar of income, and why might that make it riskier to buy?

Show answer

Cap rate = NOI ÷ Price, so a lower cap rate means buyers paid a higher price for the same dollar of income — Building A at 5% is the more expensive one; if anything goes wrong with rents or expenses, there's less return cushion to absorb the loss.

NOI (brief #48): A building collects $500,000 in rent annually. The owner pays $80,000 in property taxes, $40,000 in insurance, and $30,000 in maintenance. They also spent $60,000 replacing the roof this year. What is the NOI, and why does the roof replacement not reduce it?

Show answer

NOI = $500,000 − $80,000 − $40,000 − $30,000 = $350,000; the roof replacement is a capital expenditure — a one-time improvement to the asset — which sits below the NOI line because NOI only captures recurring operating income and expenses, not asset investments.
