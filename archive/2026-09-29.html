Today's concept: Percentage rent

1. The everyday version

Imagine you agreed to mow your neighbor's lawn for $20 a week, but you also said: "If your grass grows more than six inches, I get an extra $5 on top." The $20 is guaranteed no matter what. The extra $5 only kicks in when the lawn exceeds a certain threshold. Your neighbor pays more when their lawn is doing more — and you benefit when conditions are above normal.

2. The real version

Percentage rent works the same way in retail commercial real estate. A tenant — say, a clothing store in a shopping mall — signs a lease with two layers:

  • A base rent: a fixed dollar amount they owe every month no matter what. This is the $20 lawn payment.
  • A percentage rent clause: once the tenant's sales cross a certain dollar threshold (called the natural breakpoint — the sales level where the percentage kicks in), the landlord collects a percentage of every dollar of sales above that line.

Here is what the arithmetic looks like with round numbers:

  • Base rent: $8,000/month ($96,000/year)
  • Percentage clause: 6% of sales above $1,600,000/year
  • The tenant does $2,200,000 in sales this year

Sales above the breakpoint: $2,200,000 − $1,600,000 = $600,000

Percentage rent owed: $600,000 × 6% = $36,000

Total rent paid: $96,000 + $36,000 = $132,000

If the tenant had only done $1,400,000 in sales — below the breakpoint — the landlord collects just the $96,000 base. No extra.

Tie this to today: with DFW apartment demand surging but rents flat, landlords of retail space in high-traffic DFW corridors are watching tenant sales closely. A hot year for a restaurant or retailer in a strong-demand submarket could mean meaningful percentage rent income on top of the base.

3. Why a professional cares

A landlord underwriting (analyzing the finances of) a retail property needs to decide how much of their projected income is reliable base rent versus variable percentage rent — because a lender (bank or CMBS investor) will typically only count base rent when sizing a loan. A broker marketing a retail center with a lot of percentage rent income has to make the case that tenant sales are durable, or a buyer will discount that income heavily.

Check yourself

A boutique shoe store signs a lease with $5,000/month base rent and a 5% percentage rent clause on annual sales above $1,200,000. This year the store does $1,800,000 in sales. What is the store's total rent bill for the year?

Show answer

Sales above the breakpoint are $1,800,000 − $1,200,000 = $600,000. Percentage rent is $600,000 × 5% = $30,000. Base rent for the year is $5,000 × 12 = $60,000. Total rent = $60,000 + $30,000 = $90,000.

Review

Offering memorandum (Brief #19): A broker hands you a thick document about an office building for sale — glossy photos, a rent roll, a five-year income projection. What is one important thing that document is *not* doing for you, and why does that matter before you make an offer?

Show answer

An offering memorandum (OM) is a marketing document prepared by the seller's broker — it presents the property in the best possible light and is not independently verified. It is not telling you what could go wrong: vacancies that are about to hit, deferred maintenance, or tenants at risk of leaving. You need to do your own due diligence (independent investigation) rather than treat the OM's numbers as the truth.

Free rent and effective rent (Brief #23): A landlord advertises office space at $30/SF per year. To win the tenant, they offer 4 months of free rent on a 24-month lease. What is the effective rent per SF — the actual average the tenant pays — and why does the gap between $30 and the effective rent matter to a buyer evaluating this property?

Show answer

The tenant pays $30/SF for 20 months and $0 for 4 months. Total rent collected over 24 months = $30 × 20 = $600/SF. Spread over 24 months, the effective rent = $600 ÷ 24 = $25/SF per year. A buyer cares because the NOI (Net Operating Income — the property's actual cash income after expenses) is based on what tenants really pay, not the headline rate — so a property priced on $30/SF rents is overpriced if the actual cash coming in reflects $25/SF.
