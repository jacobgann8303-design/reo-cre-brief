Today's concept: Absorption

The everyday version

Imagine a new apartment complex opens in your college town with 200 units. In the first month, 80 people sign leases. In the second month, 60 more sign. The building is "absorbing" tenants — the empty space is getting soaked up like a sponge. If people start leaving faster than new ones sign, the sponge is squeezing back out. The speed of that fill-up tells you whether demand is strong or weak.

The real version

In commercial real estate, absorption is the net change in occupied space over a period of time. "Net" means you subtract the space that tenants moved *out of* from the space they moved *into*.

Here's the shape of the math:

> Space newly leased and occupied: 500,000 sq ft

> Space vacated (tenants left): 320,000 sq ft

> Net absorption: 500,000 − 320,000 = 180,000 sq ft

Positive net absorption means the market is filling up. Negative means more space is emptying out than getting leased.

Today's headlines give you a real example: Atlanta's industrial market posted 6.7 million square feet of net absorption in Q3 alone (Bisnow). That's an enormous positive number — manufacturers are snapping up warehouse space faster than it empties. Meanwhile, in office markets, rising *availability* (space on the market, including space tenants are trying to sublease before their lease ends) signals that net absorption is likely to turn negative — which is exactly the pressure on cap rates (the ratio of a building's income to its price) we talked about yesterday.

Why a professional cares

A lender deciding whether to finance a new warehouse will look at absorption before they write a check — if 6.7 million square feet just got absorbed in Atlanta in one quarter, there's demand to support a new project. If absorption is negative, a new building might sit half-empty, the borrower can't pay the loan, and the lender is stuck with a problem. Absorption is the earliest signal that a market is heating up or cooling off, before vacancy numbers fully catch up.

Check yourself

Atlanta's industrial market absorbed 6.7 million square feet in Q3. Suppose that same market had 2.1 million square feet of new industrial space delivered (completed and available) during Q3. After the quarter ends, did the total amount of *vacant* industrial space in Atlanta go up or go down? By how much, roughly?

Show answer

Vacant space went down by roughly 4.6 million square feet. Net absorption of 6.7M sq ft means that much space went from empty to occupied; only 2.1M sq ft of new empty space was added through new deliveries, so the net effect is 6.7M − 2.1M = 4.6M sq ft less vacancy overall.

Review

Ground lease (Brief #35): You're looking at a trophy office tower in Dallas. The seller tells you the building sold for $200 million, but the land underneath it is not included — the building owner pays $800,000 a year in rent to the landowner for the right to sit on that dirt. What is the structure called, and what's the basic risk to the building owner if the lease ever expires or gets terminated?

Show answer

This is a ground lease — the building owner holds the improvements (the structure) but not the land, paying the landowner rent to use the site. The core risk is that when the ground lease expires, the land — and often the building on it — reverts to the landowner, so the building owner can lose the asset entirely if the lease isn't renewed on acceptable terms.

CRE career ladder (Brief #45): A recruiter offers you an "analyst" role at a CRE investment firm. Your friend gets an "associate" offer at a different firm. Without knowing salaries, what is the general difference in what each of you would spend your day doing?

Show answer

As an analyst, you'd spend most of your time building financial models, pulling market data, and running the numbers that support investment decisions — it's heavy on spreadsheet work and research. As an associate, you'd still do some of that but would take on more responsibility for managing parts of a deal, coordinating due diligence (the process of verifying everything about a property before buying), and interfacing more directly with clients or partners — it's a step up in judgment and ownership of outcomes.
