Today's concept: Migration and household formation

1. The everyday version

Imagine your college town. Every August, thousands of students move in — they need beds, groceries, and apartments. If suddenly the university doubles enrollment, landlords can't build fast enough and rents jump. Now flip it: if enrollment drops 30%, half the apartments sit empty and landlords start handing out free months of rent to fill units. The number of people arriving and needing a place to sleep is everything.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, migration means people physically moving from one city or state to another. Household formation means how many new separate households get created — when two roommates split up and each gets their own place, that's one migration event creating *two* households, each needing a unit.

Texas has been one of the fastest-growing states in the country. More people moving in = more households needing apartments = more demand for the exact product MAA owns. When MAA jumped +3.91% today to $120.82, part of what investors are pricing in is continued in-migration into Texas and the Sun Belt (the broad arc of southern U.S. states from the Carolinas to California that have seen big population gains). Meanwhile today's Trepp data shows 25% of Texas floating-rate apartment loans are already strained — meaning even strong in-migration can't fully offset what high interest rates (the 10-year at 5.12%) are doing to operating margins.

Quick math to make migration concrete: if a market gains 50,000 new residents per year and the average household size is 2.5 people, that creates 50,000 ÷ 2.5 = 20,000 new households that each need a unit. If there are only 15,000 new apartments being delivered, the market is short 5,000 units — rents go up.

3. Why a professional cares

A developer deciding whether to break ground needs to know whether enough people are arriving — and staying — to fill units over the three years it takes to build. A lender underwriting (evaluating the risk of) a new apartment loan uses household formation projections to stress-test whether rents will hold if migration slows.

Show answer

Review

Cash-on-cash return (Brief #16): You put $200,000 of your own cash into a deal. At the end of the year the property sends you $14,000 in actual cash distributions (money paid out to you, before any accounting adjustments). What is your cash-on-cash return, and what does that number actually measure?

Show answer

$14,000 ÷ $200,000 = 7.0% cash-on-cash return; it measures how much actual cash you received relative to the cash you personally put in — not total profit, not appreciation, just the dollars that hit your account divided by the dollars you invested.

CAM reconciliation (Brief #28): A retail tenant signs a lease where they pay $2,000/month in estimated common area maintenance (CAM) charges — the tenant's share of costs like parking lot upkeep, landscaping, and hallway lighting in a shared building. At year-end, the landlord adds up the actual costs and finds the tenant's true share was $2,600/month. What happens at reconciliation, and who writes a check to whom?

Show answer

The tenant underpaid by $600/month × 12 months = $7,200 for the year, so at reconciliation the tenant owes the landlord $7,200; CAM reconciliation is the annual true-up that settles the difference between estimated and actual shared-cost charges.
