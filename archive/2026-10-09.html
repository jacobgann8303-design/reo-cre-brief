Today's concept: Sale-leaseback

1. The everyday version

Imagine you own your car outright and you need cash. A leasing company buys the car from you for $15,000 — but instead of taking the keys, they immediately let you keep driving it for $300 a month. You got your cash. They got an asset that pays them every month. You still have your car. Nobody moved, except the title.

2. The real version

A company that owns its headquarters building does the same thing, just with zeros added. Say a pharmaceutical company owns its office outright — no mortgage. They sell it to an investor for $50,000,000. On the same day they close, they sign a long-term lease — say, 15 years — to stay in the building and pay $3,000,000 per year in rent.

Here's the investor's math:

$3,000,000 ÷ $50,000,000 = 6.0% cap rate (the first-year income divided by the purchase price — the investor's basic return before financing costs)

Now tie this to today: the 10-year Treasury is at 5.28%. That 6.0% cap rate only beats the "risk-free" Treasury by 0.72%. That's a thin margin — the investor is taking on real estate risk for less than one percentage point of extra return. This is exactly what the CRE Daily headline flagged for apartments. The same squeeze shows up in sale-leaseback deals.

3. Why a professional cares

When Treasury rates rise, that 0.72% cushion shrinks or disappears entirely — at some point the investor can just buy a Treasury bond and do better with no hassle. That forces the sale price down (lowering the price raises the cap rate, restoring the cushion) or kills the deal. A company shopping for cash through a sale-leaseback today has to accept a lower price than it would have gotten two years ago, precisely because the 10-year is at 5.28% instead of 2%.

Check yourself

A retailer owns its store building free and clear. It sells the building for $8,000,000 and signs a 20-year lease to stay, paying $400,000 per year in rent. A year later, the 10-year Treasury rises from 5.28% to 6.50%. If an investor now needs at least 1.00% of extra return above Treasuries to justify the real estate risk, what is the maximum price a new buyer would pay for that same building with that same lease?

Show answer

The investor needs a cap rate of at least 6.50% + 1.00% = 7.50%. Cap rate = NOI ÷ Price, so Price = NOI ÷ Cap rate = $400,000 ÷ 0.075 = $5,333,333 — meaning the building's value would fall from $8,000,000 to about $5.3 million purely because rates rose, even though the rent stayed the same.

Review

Debt service coverage ratio (brief #11): A lender is looking at a small office building. The building generates $180,000 per year in net operating income — NOI, meaning rent collected minus operating expenses like taxes, insurance, and maintenance, but before any loan payments. The annual mortgage payment would be $150,000. Without knowing the definition of DSCR, can you figure out whether this lender is likely to approve the loan — and roughly what the ratio is?

Show answer

DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio) is NOI divided by the annual loan payment — it tells the lender how many times over the property's income covers what it owes. Here: $180,000 ÷ $150,000 = 1.20x. Most lenders want at least 1.25x, so this loan would likely be on the edge or turned down; the property doesn't generate quite enough income above its debt payment to satisfy a typical lender's cushion requirement.

Tenant improvement allowance and leasing commissions (brief #22): A landlord signs a new tenant to a 5-year office lease at $50,000 per year in rent. The landlord gives the tenant $80,000 in tenant improvement allowance (cash the landlord pays to build out the space the way the tenant wants it) and owes the broker a leasing commission of $25,000. How much does the landlord actually net in year one after those two costs?

Show answer

In year one, the landlord collects $50,000 in rent but must absorb the full TI allowance of $80,000 and the leasing commission of $25,000 — totaling $105,000 in upfront costs — so the landlord is actually $55,000 in the hole in year one ($50,000 − $105,000), which is why a signed lease is not the same as a profitable lease, and why these costs matter when underwriting a deal.
