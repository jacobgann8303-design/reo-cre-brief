Today's concept: Going-in vs. exit cap rate

1. The everyday version

Say you buy a used car for $10,000 and rent it out through Turo for $1,000 a year in profit. Your "return at purchase" is 10%. Three years later, used car prices have climbed and people are willing to accept a smaller return to own rental cars — so a buyer pays $14,000 for the same income stream. The return at sale is only 7.1%. You bought at a high return and sold at a low one — and that difference made you money.

2. The real version

A cap rate — short for capitalization rate — is the ratio of a property's annual net income to its price. NOI (Net Operating Income) is what's left from rent after paying operating expenses like taxes, insurance, and maintenance, but *before* paying the mortgage.

The going-in cap rate is that ratio at the moment you buy. The exit cap rate is what the market will accept when you sell years later.

Here's why the math matters. Say you buy an apartment building today for $2,000,000, with an NOI of $120,000:

> $120,000 ÷ $2,000,000 = 6.0% going-in cap rate

You plan to hold five years, grow the NOI to $150,000 through rent increases, and sell. If buyers at that future date accept a 5.5% exit cap rate — meaning they're willing to pay more per dollar of income, perhaps because interest rates have fallen from today's 4.96% — your sale price would be:

> $150,000 ÷ 0.055 = $2,727,273

But if rates stay elevated or rise further and buyers demand a 6.5% exit cap rate, your sale price shrinks:

> $150,000 ÷ 0.065 = $2,307,692

Same income growth. Nearly a $420,000 swing in what someone will pay — just from a one-point difference in exit cap rate.

3. Why a professional cares

When underwriting a deal — meaning building the financial model that justifies buying — a broker or investor will stress-test the exit cap rate. With the 10-year at 4.96% today and the Fed likely to raise rates further, assuming buyers will accept a *lower* exit cap rate than your going-in cap rate is an aggressive bet. If your deal only pencils out if cap rates compress (buyers accept smaller returns), a professional will flag that assumption immediately.

Check yourself

You buy a small office building for $3,000,000 with an NOI of $180,000. You underwrite it assuming you'll sell in four years at a 5.5% exit cap rate, by which point NOI will have grown to $200,000. Calculate your projected exit price. Then: if rising interest rates push the exit cap rate to 6.5% instead, how much value does that cost you on the sale?

Show answer

At a 5.5% exit cap rate, the projected sale price is $200,000 ÷ 0.055 = $3,636,364. At a 6.5% exit cap rate, the sale price drops to $200,000 ÷ 0.065 = $3,076,923 — a difference of roughly $559,000, all from a one-point shift in what buyers demand at exit.

Review

IRR vs. equity multiple (Brief #15): You're shown two deals. Deal A has an IRR (Internal Rate of Return — the annualized percentage return on your invested dollars, weighted for timing) of 22% over two years. Deal B has an IRR of 14% over seven years. A friend says Deal A is obviously better because the IRR is higher. What's the one number they should also look at before agreeing, and why might Deal B actually put more dollars in their pocket?

Show answer

They should look at the equity multiple — total dollars returned divided by dollars invested. A 22% IRR over two years on a $100,000 investment might return $148,000 (a 1.48x multiple), while a 14% IRR over seven years might return $257,000 (a 2.57x multiple). The IRR looks better on Deal A, but Deal B delivers far more actual cash because of the longer compounding period.

Loan-to-value vs. loan-to-cost (Brief #12): A developer buys land for $500,000 and spends $1,500,000 building on it, for a total cost of $2,000,000. An appraiser says the finished building is worth $2,600,000. A lender offers a loan of $1,560,000. What is the loan-to-cost ratio (loan divided by total cost), and what is the loan-to-value ratio (loan divided by appraised value) — and which one is lower?

Show answer

Loan-to-cost is $1,560,000 ÷ $2,000,000 = 78%. Loan-to-value is $1,560,000 ÷ $2,600,000 = 60%. LTV is lower; on a development deal, LTV is typically the more conservative figure because the appraised value already bakes in the profit the developer hopes to capture, so lenders often underwrite to LTC to limit their exposure during construction.
