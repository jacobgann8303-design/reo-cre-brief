Today's concept: Anchor tenant and co-tenancy clauses

1. The everyday version

Imagine a food court. You go because Chick-fil-A is there — that's the place you actually wanted. While you're walking in, you grab a drink from the smoothie stand next to it. Now imagine Chick-fil-A closes. You stop coming. The smoothie stand loses 60% of its customers and can barely pay rent. The smoothie stand's whole business depended on who else was in that building.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, the Chick-fil-A is called an anchor tenant — a large, well-known tenant whose presence draws traffic to everyone around them. Think Target, Whole Foods, or a big gym in a shopping center. Smaller tenants — the smoothie stand, the nail salon, the phone repair kiosk — sign leases that include a co-tenancy clause: a contract provision that says, in plain English, *"if the anchor leaves, my rent drops — or I can leave too."*

Here's why that matters with today's numbers. The 10-year Treasury is at 5.11%. When rates are that high, buyers need a property to produce more income to justify the price — they demand a higher cap rate (cap rate = annual net income ÷ purchase price; a higher cap rate means you're paying less per dollar of income). A retail strip with a strong anchor — say Whole Foods, like the Houston deal in today's headlines — might trade at a 6.5% cap rate. The same strip, same location, but with a vacant anchor space and triggered co-tenancy clauses cutting every other tenant's rent? The income collapses. Maybe net income (the money left after operating expenses — professionals call this NOI, or Net Operating Income) falls 30%. That same 6.5% cap rate now implies a price 30% lower. A $10M center becomes a $7M center, fast.

3. Why a professional cares

When a lender or buyer underwrites (evaluates the financial risk of) a retail property, they look hard at who the anchor is, how long they're locked in, and exactly what the co-tenancy clauses say — because those clauses are effectively a hidden revenue risk that only shows up when something goes wrong. With a 10-year at 5.11% and financing already expensive, a triggered co-tenancy clause can turn a viable deal into an unfinanceable one overnight.

Check yourself

A shopping center has a Whole Foods anchor and five smaller tenants. The co-tenancy clause says smaller tenants can reduce rent by 25% if the anchor goes dark (meaning the anchor closes or vacates). The center currently produces $650,000 per year in NOI (net operating income — income after operating expenses). If Whole Foods leaves and all five small tenants exercise the co-tenancy clause, and those small tenants together account for 40% of the current NOI, what is the new NOI? Should a buyer adjust the price they'd pay for this center — and in which direction?

Show answer

Small tenants produce 40% of $650,000 = $260,000. A 25% reduction on that = $65,000 lost. New NOI = $650,000 − $65,000 = $585,000. A buyer should lower the price they're willing to pay, because the income the property produces has dropped — and at the same cap rate, a lower NOI means a lower value.

Review

Going-in vs. exit cap rate (Brief #17): A developer buys an office building today and builds a financial model showing what they expect to sell it for in five years. They assume they'll buy it at a 6.5% cap rate and sell it at a 6.0% cap rate — meaning they expect buyers in five years to pay more per dollar of income than buyers do today. Given that the 10-year Treasury just jumped to 5.11%, is that exit assumption optimistic or conservative, and why does it matter?

Show answer

It's optimistic — when Treasury yields are high, buyers typically demand higher cap rates (more income per dollar of price), not lower ones; assuming a lower exit cap rate than the going-in cap rate inflates the projected sale price, which makes the deal look better on paper than it may be in reality, which is exactly the kind of assumption to challenge when reviewing someone's underwriting.

Pro forma (Brief #18): A broker hands you a one-page pro forma (a projected financial statement showing expected income and expenses) for a retail center. It shows 95% occupancy and full rent from all tenants for the next five years. You just learned the anchor tenant has a co-tenancy clause and their lease expires in 18 months. What's the problem with trusting that pro forma?

Show answer

The pro forma assumes everything stays stable, but if the anchor doesn't renew, smaller tenants may trigger their co-tenancy clauses and cut rent or leave — meaning actual income could fall well below the projected number; a pro forma is only as good as its assumptions, and this one is ignoring a real, near-term risk hiding in the lease documents.
