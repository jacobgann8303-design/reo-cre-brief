Today's concept: Triple net (NNN) vs. gross vs. modified gross

1. The everyday version

Imagine you're splitting an apartment with two friends. In one version, you pay a flat $600 a month and your landlord handles everything — electricity, water, repairs. In another version, you pay $400 but you also cover your share of utilities and help pay for anything that breaks. Same apartment. Very different deals. The question isn't just "what's the rent?" — it's "what does that rent *include*?"

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, that same question has three common answers.

Gross lease: The tenant pays one flat number. The landlord pays property taxes, insurance, and maintenance out of that rent. Think of a small office tenant paying $30 per square foot (per SF) per year — they write one check, done.

Triple net (NNN) lease: The tenant pays a lower base rent *plus* their share of three buckets — property taxes, building insurance, and maintenance/repairs (the three "nets"). A warehouse tenant might pay $12/SF in base rent, then $3/SF in taxes, $1/SF in insurance, and $2/SF in maintenance — totaling $18/SF. The landlord's check is predictable; the tenant absorbs the variable costs.

Modified gross: Somewhere between the two. Maybe the tenant pays base rent plus their electricity, but the landlord still covers taxes and insurance. You'll see this most in multi-tenant office buildings.

Here's why today's numbers make this matter right now. The 10-year Treasury is at 4.94%. When a landlord goes to refinance (replace an old loan with a new one at current rates), their borrowing cost is near that number. A landlord with gross leases has to eat any surprise expense — a spike in property taxes, a new insurance premium — out of their own pocket, which shrinks the NOI they have left to cover that higher debt payment. A landlord with NNN leases passes those surprises to the tenant. In a rising-rate environment, that difference can determine whether a refinance works.

Quick illustration: A warehouse leased NNN at $18/SF over 50,000 SF generates:

$18 × 50,000 = $900,000/year in total rent collected from the tenant.

The landlord's actual operating costs are already covered by the tenant, so nearly all of that flows through to NOI. A gross lease at the same total might leave the landlord exposed if insurance costs jump.

3. Why a professional cares

When underwriting a deal (analyzing it to decide whether to lend or invest), a lender or buyer will immediately ask: "What type of lease is this?" A NNN lease on a single-tenant building (one tenant, one lease) is treated almost like a bond — predictable, stable. A gross-leased building means the landlord is absorbing volatility. Right now, with borrowing costs near 5%, lenders are scrutinizing that volatility very carefully.

Check yourself

A property owner has a 20,000 SF office building. She charges tenants $25/SF per year on a gross lease. Property taxes run $4/SF, insurance $1/SF, and maintenance $2/SF — all paid by the landlord.

A prospective buyer offers to buy the building assuming a 7.0% cap rate (cap rate = NOI ÷ purchase price; a 7.0% cap means the buyer wants to earn $7.00 for every $100 they spend).

What is the NOI, and what price does that imply? Then: if the leases were NNN instead and the landlord kept the same $25/SF headline rent but now the *tenant* covers taxes, insurance, and maintenance — how would that change the NOI and therefore the implied price at the same 7.0% cap?

Show answer

Gross lease NOI = ($25 - $4 - $1 - $2) × 20,000 = $18 × 20,000 = $360,000. At a 7.0% cap rate, price = $360,000 ÷ 0.07 = $5,142,857. Under NNN, the landlord keeps the full $25/SF because tenants now pay expenses, so NOI = $25 × 20,000 = $500,000; at the same 7.0% cap, price = $500,000 ÷ 0.07 = $7,142,857 — about $2 million more, for the exact same building, just because of how the lease is structured.

Review

Cap rate (Brief #1): A building produces $168,000 in NOI per year and just sold for $2,100,000. What is the cap rate, and based on that number alone, would you say this building sold at a "cheap" or "expensive" price relative to a building that sold at a 5% cap rate?

Show answer

Cap rate = $168,000 ÷ $2,100,000 = 8.0%. An 8 cap is cheaper (the buyer is getting more income per dollar spent) than a 5 cap — a 5 cap means the buyer paid a much higher price for the same dollar of income, which is expensive.

NOI (Brief #2): A landlord collects $500,000 in rent this year. She pays $80,000 in property taxes, $20,000 in insurance, $30,000 in repairs, and she also replaced the roof for $150,000. What is the NOI, and does the roof replacement go above or below the NOI line — and why?

Show answer

NOI = $500,000 - $80,000 - $20,000 - $30,000 = $370,000; the $150,000 roof replacement is a capital expenditure (a large, one-time improvement) and sits *below* the NOI line because NOI only captures recurring operating income and expenses — it is not reduced by capex, which is tracked separately.
