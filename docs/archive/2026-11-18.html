Today's concept: Cash-on-cash return

1. The everyday version

Say you lend a friend $1,000 to start a small resale business. At the end of the year, they hand you back $80 in profit — not the original $1,000, just the cash you earned on top of it. You divided $80 by $1,000 and got 8%. That percentage — cash you actually received this year divided by cash you actually put in — is cash-on-cash return. It ignores what the business might be worth someday. It just answers: *what did my dollars do for me this year?*

2. The real version

Now put that shape into a real deal. Say you buy a small apartment building. The purchase price is $2,000,000. You put in $600,000 of your own money as a down payment (that's your cash in — called equity, meaning the portion you own outright). You borrow the rest as a mortgage. After paying the mortgage, property taxes, insurance, and maintenance, the property throws off $42,000 in cash to you over the year.

$42,000 ÷ $600,000 = 7.0% cash-on-cash return.

Now look at today's market: the 10-year Treasury is at 5.12%. A Treasury is essentially risk-free — the U.S. government won't miss a payment. So your apartment deal is giving you 7.0% on cash you can lose versus 5.12% on cash you basically cannot lose. The gap is about 1.9 percentage points. Whether that gap is enough reward for the risk is the judgment call. Notice this is different from the cap rate (Net Operating Income ÷ purchase price) you learned earlier — cap rate ignores your mortgage. Cash-on-cash uses the actual cash left after the mortgage payment, divided by only the equity you wrote a check for.

3. Why a professional cares

Cash-on-cash is the number that tells an investor whether their down payment is working hard enough *right now* — before any future sale. When interest rates rise (as they have been), mortgage payments go up, the leftover cash shrinks, and cash-on-cash falls, which is exactly why deals that penciled at 3% rates look broken at 5.12%.

Check yourself

A deal has these facts: purchase price $1,500,000, down payment $450,000, mortgage and all expenses paid. After everything, $27,000 in cash lands in your account at year-end. The 10-year Treasury yields 5.12% today.

What is the cash-on-cash return, and would a rational investor be excited about this deal given where risk-free rates are?

Show answer

$27,000 ÷ $450,000 = 6.0% cash-on-cash return. The 10-year Treasury is yielding 5.12% with essentially no risk, so this deal offers only about 0.88 percentage points of extra return for taking on real estate risk — most investors would want a wider gap than that, and many would pass or negotiate a lower price.

Review

Question 1 — 10-year Treasury and cap rate spread (Brief #51):

You hear that the 10-year Treasury yield jumped from 4.5% to 5.12% over a few months. Without anything else changing, what does that typically do to commercial property values, and what is the mechanism connecting the two?

Show answer

Rising Treasury yields push cap rates (NOI ÷ price) higher, because investors demand more return from real estate when they can get more from risk-free bonds; a higher cap rate on the same NOI means a lower price, so property values fall — the spread between Treasuries and cap rates is the market's risk premium, and when the floor rises, the whole structure rises with it.

Question 2 — Replacement cost (Brief #41):

A developer is looking at an existing warehouse that rents for $12 per square foot per year. She runs the numbers and finds that building a brand-new identical warehouse would cost the equivalent of $9 per square foot per year in rent to justify the construction cost. What does replacement cost tell her about rent growth in that market, and what does it suggest about the existing building's competitive position?

Show answer

Replacement cost sets a ceiling on rents — if existing space already rents above what it costs to justify new construction, developers will build new supply, which caps how much further rents can rise; in this case, at $12 versus a $9 justification cost, the market is well above replacement cost, meaning new supply is likely coming, which will eventually pressure the existing building's rents and occupancy.
