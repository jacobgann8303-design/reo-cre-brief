Today's concept: Cap rate

1. The everyday version

Imagine you're deciding whether to buy a vending machine for $10,000. The machine spits out $700 in profit every year after you pay for restocking and repairs. To figure out whether that's a good deal, you'd naturally ask: what percentage return am I getting on my $10,000? You divide the annual profit by the price you paid: $700 ÷ $10,000 = 7%. That percentage — what your investment earns in a year relative to what you paid — is the core idea.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, that same calculation has a name: the capitalization rate, or cap rate. The "profit" is called NOI — Net Operating Income — which is the rent a property collects in a year minus the costs of running it (maintenance, insurance, property management, taxes), but *before* you count mortgage payments. The price is whatever someone paid for the building.

Here's a real shape using today's environment. Say a small office building in Fort Worth generates $168,000 in NOI per year and sells for $2,400,000.

$168,000 ÷ $2,400,000 = 0.070 = 7.0% cap rate.

Now look at the 10-year Treasury, sitting at 5.00% today. That's what the U.S. government will pay you, risk-free, to lend it money for ten years. A Fort Worth office building at 7.0% pays you 2 percentage points more — but you're taking on real risk: tenants could leave, the roof could need replacing, the market could soften. That extra return above the risk-free rate is the reward for taking that risk. Professionals call that gap the risk premium — the extra percentage you demand for doing something harder than buying a Treasury.

3. Why a professional cares

Cap rate is the single fastest way to compare properties and to sense-check a price. If someone asks $3,000,000 for that same building with $168,000 NOI, the cap rate drops to 5.6% — and suddenly you're barely clearing the risk-free Treasury rate for taking on all that property risk. A deal that *felt* fine at 7.0% may not pencil at 5.6%. When the 10-year rises (as it has, sitting at 5.00% today), buyers demand higher cap rates to maintain their risk premium — which means they'll pay *less* for the same building. That's why rising rates and falling property values tend to travel together.

Check yourself

A small retail strip center produces $220,000 in NOI per year. An investor offers $3,100,000 for it. The 10-year Treasury is at 5.00%.

Question: What is the cap rate on this deal, and would you say the risk premium — the extra return above the Treasury — is thin, reasonable, or generous? Show your arithmetic.

Show answer

$220,000 ÷ $3,100,000 = 7.1% cap rate. The risk premium is 7.1% − 5.0% = 2.1 percentage points above the risk-free rate, which most professionals would call thin-to-reasonable in a higher-rate environment — you're earning only 2.1% extra for taking on leasing risk, maintenance risk, and illiquidity compared to simply buying a Treasury bond.

Review

REIT structure (Brief #30): REITs are required to distribute at least 90% of their taxable income to shareholders each year. What does that requirement force them to do when they want to buy another building or fund a major renovation — and why can't they just save up their profits the way a regular company might?

Show answer

Because they must pay out at least 90% of taxable income as dividends, REITs can't accumulate much cash internally; to fund acquisitions or big capital projects, they must go to outside markets — issuing new shares or taking on debt — rather than simply retaining earnings like a typical corporation.

Submarket (Brief #39): If someone says "the Dallas office market is softening," why is that statement almost useless for an investor trying to decide whether to buy a specific building in Uptown Dallas?

Show answer

"Dallas" is not one market — it contains dozens of distinct submarkets (Uptown, Las Colinas, Downtown, Frisco, etc.) with different vacancy rates, rent trends, and tenant demand; a building in Uptown could be tightening while one in a suburban corridor softens, so city-level data hides the variation that actually drives investment decisions.
