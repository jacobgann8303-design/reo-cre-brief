Today's concept: The 10-year Treasury and cap rate spread

1. The everyday version

Imagine your friend asks to borrow $1,000 from you. You could instead put that money in a savings account guaranteed to pay you 4.67% a year with zero risk of losing it. So if your friend wants to borrow the money instead, you're going to demand more than 4.67% — because your friend might not pay you back, and that risk deserves extra compensation. The gap between what the guaranteed option pays and what you demand from the risky one is the *spread* — the extra return for taking on extra risk.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, the "guaranteed option" is the 10-year U.S. Treasury bond, because the U.S. government has never failed to pay. Today that rate is 4.67%.

The "risky option" is buying a commercial property. The return a property produces relative to its purchase price is called the cap rate — short for capitalization rate. Here's the arithmetic:

If a building earns $168,000 per year in net operating income — NOI, meaning rent collected minus operating expenses like taxes, insurance, and maintenance, but before paying any mortgage — and you buy it for $2,400,000, then:

$168,000 ÷ $2,400,000 = 7.0% cap rate

The spread is the gap between those two numbers:

7.0% − 4.67% = 2.33 percentage points (233 basis points)

That 2.33% is the extra return you're getting above the Treasury for owning a physical building with real tenants, maintenance headaches, and no government guarantee behind it. Professionals call this the cap rate spread.

3. Why a professional cares

When the 10-year Treasury rises — as it has been doing — the spread shrinks unless property prices fall or rents rise to push the cap rate up to match. A shrinking spread means buyers are accepting less compensation for taking on risk, which makes deals harder to justify. When the spread gets too thin, buyers stop bidding, prices drop, and deals freeze. That's exactly why the CBRE headline today says the outlook for cap rates is "more guarded" — higher Treasuries are forcing the math to adjust.

Check yourself

Today's 10-year Treasury is 4.67%. Suppose a well-located industrial warehouse in Fort Worth sells at a 5.2% cap rate. A year ago, when the 10-year was 3.50%, the same type of building traded at a 5.8% cap rate. Which environment gave buyers *more* extra compensation for owning real estate versus the safe government bond, and what happened to property prices as the spread changed?

Show answer

A year ago, the spread was 5.8% − 3.50% = 2.3 percentage points; today the spread is 5.2% − 4.67% = 0.53 percentage points — so a year ago buyers earned far more extra compensation for owning real estate. As the Treasury rose and the spread compressed, buyers were getting less reward for the same risk, so to restore an acceptable spread, prices had to fall (pushing cap rates back up) — meaning the building is worth less today than a year ago even if the rent it earns is unchanged.
