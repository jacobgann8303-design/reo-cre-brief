Today's concept: The capital stack

1. The everyday version

Imagine you and two friends are buying a $10,000 used car. You put in $2,000 of your own cash. A family member lends you $5,000 and says, "Pay me back first, before anyone else gets anything." Your other friend lends you the remaining $3,000 but agrees to wait in line behind the family member. If the car gets totaled and the insurance pays out $6,000, the family member gets their $5,000 first, your other friend gets the last $1,000, and you get nothing. Who took the most risk? You did — your $2,000 is the first money to disappear in a bad outcome.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, this layered structure of money is called the capital stack — literally, a stack of different types of financing piled on top of each other, with the safest money on the bottom and the riskiest on top.

Take a real example built from today's numbers. Suppose a developer buys an apartment building in Dallas (where the pipeline is leading the nation, per CRE Daily today) for $10,000,000.

| Layer | Amount | Position |

|---|---|---|

| Senior debt (bank loan) | $6,500,000 | Bottom — paid back first |

| Mezzanine debt ("mezz" — a second loan, riskier than the bank's) | $1,500,000 | Middle |

| Equity (the developer's own cash) | $2,000,000 | Top — last to get paid |

The senior lender, priced off the 10-year Treasury at 4.78% plus a spread (extra percentage the lender charges for the risk of lending on real estate rather than a Treasury bond), might charge 6.5% interest. The mezz lender, taking more risk, might charge 10–12%. The developer's equity earns whatever is left over — potentially a lot if the deal works, potentially nothing if it doesn't.

The arithmetic of the stack: $6,500,000 + $1,500,000 + $2,000,000 = $10,000,000. Every dollar of the purchase price has to come from somewhere in that stack. Full stop.

3. Why a professional cares

Where you sit in the stack determines both your risk and your expected return — and when the 10-year Treasury rises (as it has, to 4.78%), the senior debt gets more expensive, which squeezes every layer above it. If the senior loan costs more, the mezz lender needs to charge even more to justify their risk, and the equity return shrinks — sometimes until the deal no longer makes sense to build.

Check yourself

A developer is buying a $20,000,000 office building in Dallas. The senior lender will loan 65% of the purchase price (that is the LTV — Loan-to-Value ratio, meaning the loan divided by the property's value). A mezz lender will cover half of the remaining gap. The developer covers the rest with equity.

Work out: How many dollars does each layer of the capital stack contribute?

Show answer

Senior debt = 65% × $20,000,000 = $13,000,000. Remaining gap = $20,000,000 − $13,000,000 = $7,000,000. Mezz covers half = $3,500,000. Developer equity = $3,500,000. Stack: $13,000,000 senior + $3,500,000 mezz + $3,500,000 equity = $20,000,000.

Review

Vacancy vs. availability (Brief #7): A landlord tells you a building is 8% vacant but 14% available. Those two numbers are different — what does each one actually count, and which one should you trust more if you're trying to understand how much space is really competing for tenants right now?

Show answer

Vacancy counts only space that is physically empty and not being paid for; availability counts all space being marketed, including space still occupied by tenants who are leaving or subletting. Availability is the more useful number for understanding real competition — it shows how much space will soon be chasing tenants even if it isn't empty yet.

Loan-to-value vs. loan-to-cost (Brief #12): A developer is building a new apartment project from scratch. The construction cost is $8,000,000, but an appraiser says the finished building will be worth $10,000,000. A lender offers a loan equal to 70% of cost. Why would the lender use cost rather than the appraised value, and how big is the loan?

Show answer

Lenders use cost on construction deals because the value doesn't exist yet — the appraised value is a projection that might not materialize, so cost is the safer, verifiable anchor. Loan = 70% × $8,000,000 = $5,600,000; if the lender had used value instead it would have been $7,000,000, which is $1,400,000 more exposure on a building not yet built.
