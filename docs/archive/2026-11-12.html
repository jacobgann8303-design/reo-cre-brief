Today's concept: Loan-to-value and loan-to-cost

The everyday version

Imagine you want to buy a used truck listed at $20,000. You have $5,000 saved, so you go to the bank and ask to borrow the other $15,000. The bank looks at the truck, agrees it's worth $20,000, and calculates that your loan is 75% of the truck's value — that's your loan-to-value. Now imagine the truck needs $2,000 in repairs before you can drive it. Your total cost to get it running is $22,000, and that changes what a lender might think about how much you're really putting in.

The real version

Loan-to-value (LTV) and loan-to-cost (LTC) are the same idea applied to commercial real estate.

Loan-to-value (LTV): How much you borrowed divided by what the property is worth today.

Say Broe Real Estate just acquired that 345-unit apartment tower in Uptown Dallas. Suppose the purchase price — which typically reflects market value — was $80 million, and they borrowed $56 million. Here's the math:

$56,000,000 ÷ $80,000,000 = 70% LTV

That means the lender is covering 70 cents of every dollar of value. The borrower is putting in 30 cents — that's their equity cushion (the money they'd lose first if things go wrong, which is what makes lenders feel safe).

Loan-to-cost (LTC): How much you borrowed divided by what it actually costs to build or acquire and improve the property — not just the market value. This one matters most in construction and value-add deals, where you're spending money before the property is worth what you paid to build it.

Say a developer is building a Houston industrial project (like the 749,016 SF Rankin 59 project in the headlines). Total cost to build: $60 million. They borrow $45 million.

$45,000,000 ÷ $60,000,000 = 75% LTC

Now notice: with the 10-year Treasury at 4.88%, lenders are nervous. Higher rates mean higher borrowing costs, which means the income a property generates has to work harder to cover the loan payments. When lenders get nervous, they lower the LTV they'll allow — maybe from 70% down to 60% — which means the borrower has to put in more of their own money.

Why a professional cares

LTV is the number lenders use to decide how much risk they're taking — if a property's value drops, lenders want to know they can still recover the loan by selling. When rising rates push property values down (which they tend to do — a concept called cap rate expansion), LTVs that looked fine a year ago can suddenly look dangerous, triggering loan defaults or forcing owners to inject more equity. That's exactly what Trepp is describing in today's headline about office loans.

Free rent and effective rent (brief #23): A landlord tells you a space rents for $30 per square foot per year — but they're offering three months free on a three-year lease. Without doing the full math, explain why $30/SF is not what the landlord actually earns per year, and what the number you'd actually want to calculate is called.

Show answer

The landlord collects zero rent for three of the thirty-six months, so they're only receiving payment for thirty-three months; spreading that over all thirty-six months gives a lower annual rate — that true blended rate is called the effective rent, and it's always lower than the stated headline rent when any free rent is involved.
