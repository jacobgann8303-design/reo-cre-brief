Today's concept: NOI

### 1. The everyday version

Imagine you own a vending machine. It takes in $500 a month from people buying snacks. But you pay $80 a month to restock it, $20 to have someone service it, and $50 for the space where it sits. Your actual take-home from running that machine — before you think about any loan you took out to buy it — is $350. That $350 is what the machine actually produces. You haven't paid yourself back for buying the machine yet. You're just counting what the operation earns.

### 2. The real version

In commercial real estate, that same idea has a name: Net Operating Income, or NOI. It's the money a property brings in from tenants — called gross income, meaning total rent collected — minus the costs of running the property day to day. Those operating costs include things like property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and property management fees. What is *not* included: any mortgage payment (called debt service — the principal and interest you pay a lender). NOI is the income the property produces before the bank gets its cut.

Here's why today's number matters. The Dallas office building in the headlines is seeking a $250 million CMBS loan. To underwrite that loan — meaning to evaluate whether it's safe to make — a lender's first question is: what is the NOI on this building? Say the building produces $17.5 million in NOI per year.

$17,500,000 ÷ $250,000,000 = 7.0% cap rate

Cap rate, as you learned last time, is NOI divided by property value. The lender uses NOI to decide if the property earns enough to cover the loan payments — especially now, with the 10-year Treasury at 4.74% and borrowing costs elevated.

### 3. Why a professional cares

If NOI drops — say a major tenant moves out and rent falls — the property is suddenly worth less and may not cover its debt payments, which is exactly the stress playing out in the office headlines right now. Conversely, if a landlord raises rents or cuts expenses and NOI rises, the property is worth more even if nothing else changes. NOI is the single number that connects a building's day-to-day operations to its market value and its ability to carry debt.

Check yourself

A small apartment complex collects $240,000 per year in rent. The owner pays $30,000 in property taxes, $12,000 in insurance, $18,000 in maintenance, and $720 per month on a mortgage. What is the NOI?

Work it out before you look.

Show answer

NOI is $180,000. You add up the operating expenses — $30,000 + $12,000 + $18,000 = $60,000 — and subtract them from the $240,000 gross income: $240,000 − $60,000 = $180,000. The mortgage payment ($720/month = $8,640/year) is debt service, not an operating expense, so it does not get subtracted when calculating NOI.
