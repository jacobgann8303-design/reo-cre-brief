Today's concept: Ground lease

1. The everyday version

Imagine you own a plot of land in a prime spot but you don't want to build anything on it yourself. Instead, you let someone else build a restaurant there — but they're renting the dirt from you. They own the building they put up; you still own the land underneath. Every month they pay you rent just for the ground. When the lease eventually ends, the building often comes back to you.

2. The real version

That's a ground lease: the landowner (called the fee owner or ground lessor) keeps the land, and the developer or tenant (the ground lessee) builds on top and operates the property, paying annual ground rent for the privilege.

Here's why today's numbers make this interesting. Ground rent is typically set as a percentage of the land's value — often pegged to the 10-year Treasury plus a spread (a spread is the extra percentage added on top as compensation for risk). With the 10-year at 5.22%, a ground lease might be priced at something like 5.22% + 1.5% = 6.72% of land value per year.

Say the land under a retail building in Dallas is worth $5,000,000:

> $5,000,000 × 6.72% = $336,000 per year in ground rent

The developer who builds on top must pay that $336,000 before they see a dollar of profit. They're building equity in the structure, but they never own the dirt.

3. Why a professional cares

Ground leases let a developer get into a premium location without buying the land — which can dramatically reduce the upfront capital (money) needed. But if the 10-year Treasury rises and ground rent resets upward at lease renewal, the economics of the whole project can flip from profitable to painful. Right now, with the 10-year at 5.22% and a Fed official hinting rates could go higher, anyone signing a ground lease with a floating reset clause needs to model what happens if that rate climbs another 50 or 100 basis points.

Check yourself

A developer is negotiating a ground lease on land valued at $8,000,000. The ground rent is set at the 10-year Treasury (currently 5.22%) plus a 1.25% spread. The property generates $700,000 per year in NOI (Net Operating Income — the income left after operating expenses but before debt payments). After paying ground rent, how much NOI is left, and does this deal look viable?

Show answer

The ground rent rate is 5.22% + 1.25% = 6.47%; applied to $8,000,000 that's $8,000,000 × 0.0647 = $517,600 per year in ground rent. Subtracting from NOI: $700,000 − $517,600 = $182,400 remaining — thin, but positive; a professional would ask whether that cushion survives a rate increase or a dip in occupancy before signing.

Review

CMBS (taught in brief #29): Trepp is one of the main data sources that tracks CMBS. In one sentence, explain what CMBS is and why a CRE lender would use it instead of a regular bank loan.

Show answer

CMBS — Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities — are bundles of commercial real estate loans that get packaged together and sold as bonds to investors on the open market; a lender uses this structure to get the loan off its books quickly and recycle capital into new loans, while the borrower gets access to debt that might not otherwise be available from a traditional bank.

Loan-to-value vs. loan-to-cost (taught in brief #12): A developer buys land for $2,000,000 and plans to spend $8,000,000 building on it, for a total cost of $10,000,000. When complete, an appraiser says the finished property is worth $13,000,000. A lender offers a loan of $7,500,000. Without needing to remember the exact terms, calculate both ratios and explain in one sentence why they give different numbers.

Show answer

Loan-to-cost (LTC) = $7,500,000 ÷ $10,000,000 = 75%, measuring the loan against what the developer actually spends; loan-to-value (LTV) = $7,500,000 ÷ $13,000,000 = 57.7%, measuring it against what the market says the finished asset is worth — they differ because construction can create value above and beyond cost, and lenders watch both figures because LTC gauges build-phase risk while LTV gauges whether the completed asset secures the debt.
