Today's concept: Vacancy vs. availability

1. The everyday version

Imagine your apartment complex has 100 units. Ten of them are sitting completely empty right now — no lease, no tenant, no furniture. But five *more* units have tenants who are moving out at the end of the month, and the landlord already knows those will be empty soon. Those five aren't empty yet, but the landlord is already advertising them.

How many units are actually open to a new renter? Ten? Or fifteen?

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, vacancy is the share of space with no tenant at all — the lights are off, the lease is gone. Availability is bigger: it includes vacant space *plus* space that's still occupied but already listed for lease — maybe the tenant is moving out, or they're subletting their extra floor.

Here's why it matters with a real number. The Cushman & Wakefield headquarters in Chicago just sold at a 45% discount to its prior value. Suppose that 31-story tower has 500,000 square feet total. If 50,000 square feet are vacant (empty, no lease), that's a 10% vacancy rate:

50,000 ÷ 500,000 = 10.0% vacant

But say another 75,000 square feet are still technically leased to tenants who are leaving in 60 days and have already listed their space for sublease (sublease = a current tenant re-renting their space to someone else). Now availability looks like this:

(50,000 + 75,000) ÷ 500,000 = 25.0% available

Same building. 10% vacant, 25% available. A new tenant shopping for space would see 25%, not 10%.

3. Why a professional cares

Availability is the number a leasing broker (the person paid to find tenants for a building) watches because it predicts where vacancy is *going* — if availability spikes, vacancy is about to follow. An investor pricing a building right now, with the 10-year at 4.95% already pressing cap rates (cap rate = a building's annual income divided by its price — higher rates push cap rates up and prices down), needs to know the real supply of competing space, not just what's empty today.

Check yourself

A downtown office building has 200,000 square feet. Right now, 20,000 square feet are completely empty. Another 30,000 square feet are leased to a law firm that signed a sublease listing last week — they're still paying rent but actively trying to get out of their lease. A broker tells you, "vacancy is 10%." Is that the number you'd use to decide whether to sign a new lease in this building? What is the availability rate, and why does it change your thinking?

Show answer

No — 10% vacancy understates the real picture. Availability is (20,000 + 30,000) ÷ 200,000 = 25%. That means one in four square feet is competing for the same pool of tenants you'd be joining, which gives you more negotiating leverage on rent and tells you the building may have a harder time filling up than the vacancy number suggests.

Review

Sale-leaseback (from brief #34): A company owns the building its business operates from. It needs cash. Without moving out, what deal could it make with a real estate investor — and what does each side give up and get?

Show answer

The company sells the building to an investor and immediately signs a long-term lease to stay as a tenant — it gets a lump sum of cash now but loses ownership and becomes obligated to pay rent going forward; the investor gets a building with a guaranteed tenant already in place.

Broker vs. principal vs. advisor (from brief #44): Three people are in the room for a CRE deal. One owns the building and is selling it. One is paid a commission only if the deal closes. One is paid a flat fee to give advice regardless of outcome. Which role is which — and whose advice is structurally the most independent?

Show answer

The owner is the principal (they have their own money on the line), the commission-paid person is the broker (their pay depends on the deal happening, which can bias their advice), and the flat-fee person is the advisor — structurally the most independent because they get paid the same whether the deal closes or not.
