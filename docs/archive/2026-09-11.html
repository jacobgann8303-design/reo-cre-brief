Today's concept: IRR vs. equity multiple

1. The everyday version

Imagine you loan your friend $1,000. In Scenario A, he pays you back $2,000 after five years. In Scenario B, he pays you back $2,000 after one year. You doubled your money in both cases. But Scenario B is obviously better — your money came back faster, so you could do something else with it sooner. Those two ideas — *how much* you multiplied your money, and *how fast* — are what IRR and equity multiple are measuring.

2. The real version

The equity multiple (also called the EM) answers the "how much" question. It's simple division:

> Total cash you got back ÷ Total cash you put in

Say you invest $500,000 in an apartment deal. Over five years, you collect distributions (your share of rental income) totaling $150,000, and when the building sells you receive $850,000. Total back: $1,000,000.

$1,000,000 ÷ $500,000 = 2.0x equity multiple. You doubled your money.

The IRR — Internal Rate of Return — answers the "how fast" question. It's the annual percentage return that would explain *exactly* the timing and size of every cash flow. You don't calculate it by hand (you use Excel or a financial calculator), but here's the shape: if that same 2.0x came back in 2 years instead of 5, your IRR would be roughly 41% instead of roughly 15%. Same multiple, wildly different IRR. With the 10-year Treasury sitting at 4.83% today, a sponsor promising a 15% IRR is offering about 10 percentage points above the "risk-free" rate (the return you'd get from lending money to the U.S. government). That spread — the extra return above the Treasury — is what investors use to decide whether the risk of owning real estate is worth it.

3. Why a professional cares

A deal can show a great equity multiple but a mediocre IRR if it takes a long time — and in a world where borrowing costs are at 4.83% on the safe end, slow deals become losers fast. Investors use IRR to compare a five-year real estate deal against a two-year one, or against a stock, on an apples-to-apples basis that the equity multiple alone can't give them.

Check yourself

You invest $200,000 in a small retail deal. You receive no distributions during the hold, and after three years you sell and get back $300,000. A friend gets the same $300,000 back on the same $200,000 investment, but it takes them seven years. Who has the better equity multiple, and who has the better IRR — and why?

Show answer

Both have the same equity multiple — $300,000 ÷ $200,000 = 1.5x — because they each tripled their money on the same starting investment. But you have the better IRR, because you got that same return in 3 years instead of 7; your money came back faster, leaving more time to reinvest it elsewhere, which is exactly what IRR rewards.

Review

Class A / B / C (taught in brief #9): You're walking through two office buildings. One was built in 1978, has older HVAC, and rents for $22 per square foot. The other was built in 2019, has floor-to-ceiling glass and a rooftop terrace, and rents for $48 per square foot. Without knowing the city, explain how you'd assign a class grade to each — and what "class" actually means.

Show answer

Class is a relative grade within a specific market, not an absolute standard — a Class A building in Lubbock might be Class B in Houston. The 2019 building with premium finishes and high rents would likely be Class A in its market; the 1978 building with dated systems and lower rents would likely be Class B or C. The grade reflects age, condition, amenities, and what tenants it attracts, compared to competing buildings nearby.

Leasable vs. rentable vs. usable square feet (taught in brief #10): A tenant signs a lease for 5,000 rentable square feet in an office building. Their actual private office space measures 4,350 square feet. What explains the gap, and what is the term for the ratio that captures it?

Show answer

The gap comes from the load factor (also called the add-on factor or common area factor), which allocates a share of shared spaces — lobbies, hallways, restrooms, mechanical rooms — to each tenant proportionally. The tenant pays rent on 5,000 rentable square feet even though they physically occupy only 4,350 usable square feet; the difference (650 SF) is their assigned share of the building's common areas.
