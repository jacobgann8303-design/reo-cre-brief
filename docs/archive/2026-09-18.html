Today's concept: Due diligence period

1. The everyday version

Imagine you find a used car you want to buy. The seller agrees on a price, you hand over a small deposit to hold it, but you get ten days to take it to your mechanic, check the Carfax, and make sure nothing's hidden under the hood. If the mechanic finds a cracked engine block, you walk away and get your deposit back. If everything checks out, you hand over the rest of the money. That window — between "we agreed on a price" and "you actually own it" — is the due diligence period.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, the same window exists between when a buyer and seller sign a purchase contract and when the deal actually closes. The buyer puts down earnest money — a deposit, often 1–2% of the purchase price — to show they're serious. They then get a fixed number of days (commonly 30 to 60) to inspect everything: the physical building, the leases, the financials, the title, the environmental reports, and the loan terms.

Here's why today's numbers make this urgent. With the 10-year Treasury at 5.01%, a buyer who signed a contract 45 days ago — when the 10-year was lower — may now find that the loan they planned on costs more than they budgeted. If they underwrote (meaning: built their financial model assuming) a 6.5% interest rate and lenders are now quoting 7.0%, their monthly debt payment on a $10 million loan jumps by roughly:

  • At 6.5%: monthly interest ≈ $10,000,000 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = $54,167
  • At 7.0%: monthly interest ≈ $10,000,000 × 0.070 ÷ 12 = $58,333

That's $4,166 more per month — and it might flip the deal from profitable to unprofitable. The due diligence period is their legal window to catch that and decide whether to proceed or walk.

3. Why a professional cares

If the buyer finds a deal-killer during due diligence — bad financials, a structural defect, a loan that no longer pencils — they can terminate the contract and get their earnest money back, as long as they act before the deadline. Once the due diligence period expires and they go "hard" (meaning their deposit becomes non-refundable), walking away costs them real money.

Check yourself

A buyer signs a contract to purchase a small office building for $2,000,000 on September 1st, with a 30-day due diligence period and $40,000 in earnest money. During due diligence, they discover the building's HVAC (heating and cooling system) needs full replacement, costing $120,000 — a cost not reflected in the seller's asking price. It is now September 28th.

Do they still have time to walk away with their deposit? And if they wait until October 3rd to decide, what changes?

Show answer

Yes — September 28th is still within the 30-day window (which runs through October 1st), so they can terminate and recover all $40,000 of earnest money; but if they wait until October 3rd, the due diligence period has expired, their deposit has gone "hard," and walking away means losing the full $40,000.

Review

Equity waterfall and promote (from brief #14): A real estate deal closes and the property is sold at a profit. The sponsor — the person who found the deal and managed it — wants to be paid more than just their ownership percentage. What is the mechanism that allows them to earn that extra cut, and what is it called when they actually receive it?

Show answer

The mechanism is the equity waterfall — a set of rules that distributes profits in a specific order, first returning capital to investors, then paying a preferred return (a promised minimum annual return to investors), and only then splitting remaining profits; the sponsor's extra share of profits above that threshold is called the promote (or carried interest).

Offering memorandum (from brief #19): A broker sends you an OM (Offering Memorandum — the marketing document a broker prepares when a property is for sale) with a pro forma (a projected income statement showing expected future rents and expenses) showing $200,000 in net operating income (NOI — the money left after operating expenses, before debt payments). Why should you not simply accept that $200,000 figure when building your own offer price?

Show answer

The broker's pro forma reflects the seller's best-case assumptions — occupancy, rent growth, and expense levels that may be optimistic — and the broker is paid by the seller, so their incentive is to make the property look as valuable as possible; a buyer must re-underwrite (rebuild the numbers from scratch using their own, more conservative assumptions) to arrive at a price that reflects realistic risk.
