Today's concept: Free rent and effective rent

1. The everyday version

Imagine you're apartment hunting and a landlord says: "Sign a 12-month lease, and the first two months are on me — no rent." You're paying $1,000 a month, but only for 10 of the 12 months. You actually hand over $10,000 across the year, not $12,000. The "sticker price" of your apartment is $1,000 a month, but what you're really paying — averaged out — is less.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, landlords do the same thing to attract tenants. It's called free rent — a period at the start of a lease when the tenant pays nothing. The actual cost to the tenant (or actual income to the landlord) when you average it across the full lease is called effective rent — what the deal is really worth per month, after all the giveaways.

Here's the arithmetic. Say King & Spalding signed that 52,826 SF Uptown Dallas lease at $40 per square foot per year, with 6 months of free rent on a 10-year (120-month) lease.

  • Gross rent over full term: $40 × 52,826 SF × 10 years = $21,130,400
  • Free rent giveaway: $40 × 52,826 SF × 0.5 years = $1,056,520
  • Net rent collected: $21,130,400 − $1,056,520 = $20,073,880
  • Effective rent per SF per year: $20,073,880 ÷ 52,826 SF ÷ 10 years = $38.00/SF/year

The quoted rate is $40. The effective rent — what the landlord actually collects — is $38.

3. Why a professional cares

When a landlord reports "asking rent" to a broker or a lender, they use the face rate ($40). But a buyer underwriting that building — figuring out what it's worth to purchase — needs the effective rent ($38), because that's the cash that will actually flow in. A big free-rent package makes a building look more expensive to a buyer while making it look attractive to a tenant — knowing the difference is how you avoid being fooled by either number.

Check yourself

A landlord signs a tenant to a 5-year lease at $30/SF/year on 10,000 SF, with 3 months of free rent upfront. What is the effective rent per SF per year?

Show answer

Total gross rent = $30 × 10,000 × 5 = $1,500,000. Free rent = $30 × 10,000 × 0.25 years = $75,000. Net rent = $1,500,000 − $75,000 = $1,425,000. Effective rent = $1,425,000 ÷ 10,000 SF ÷ 5 years = $28.50/SF/year.

Review

Price per square foot (Brief #3): You're looking at two industrial buildings — one is 20,000 SF that sold for $2,000,000, and another is 8,000 SF that sold for $900,000. Which one sold for more per square foot, and why can that number mislead you when comparing the two deals?

Show answer

Building 1 sold for $100/SF ($2,000,000 ÷ 20,000); Building 2 sold for $112.50/SF ($900,000 ÷ 8,000), so Building 2 was pricier per square foot — but price per SF ignores condition, lease terms, location quality, and age, so a higher $/SF doesn't automatically mean it was the worse deal.

Cash-on-cash return (Brief #16): You put $200,000 of your own cash into a property (the rest was borrowed). In the first year the property puts $14,000 in your pocket after paying the mortgage and all expenses. What is your cash-on-cash return — that is, the return on the actual dollars you invested — and what does that number tell you that a cap rate doesn't?

Show answer

Cash-on-cash return = $14,000 ÷ $200,000 = 7.0%; unlike a cap rate (which ignores debt and measures the property's income against its full price), cash-on-cash tells you the actual yield on the dollars you personally wrote a check for, so it reflects the impact of your specific financing terms.
