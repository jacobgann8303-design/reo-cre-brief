Today's concept: Equity waterfall and promote

1. The everyday version

Imagine you and a friend open a food truck together. You put up $40,000; your friend handles all the operations. You agree upfront: first, you get your $40,000 back. Then you both split profits 80/20 — 80% to you, 20% to your friend — until you've made a 10% annual return on your money. After that, once you're taken care of, the split flips: 50/50 going forward. Your friend works hard to get you past that threshold fast, because the flip is where they finally make real money. That flip — that extra slice your friend earns for hitting the target — is the promote.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, the two parties are usually a limited partner (LP) — the passive investor who writes the check, like a pension fund or wealthy individual — and a general partner (GP), also called the sponsor, who finds the deal and runs it.

Here's a simplified waterfall on a $10 million apartment acquisition:

  • Return of capital: LP gets their $7 million equity investment back first.
  • Preferred return (a minimum annual return promised to the LP before the GP sees profit): LP gets 8% per year — $560,000/year — until that hurdle is cleared. $7,000,000 × 0.08 = $560,000/year.
  • Catch-up: GP gets a higher share briefly to "catch up" to their promoted interest.
  • Promote: Above the 8% hurdle, profits split 70% to the LP and 30% to the GP — even though the GP may have put in little or no money.

With today's 10-year at 4.80%, that 8% preferred return has to clear a high bar — because a passive investor can earn 4.80% in a risk-free Treasury. The spread between 4.80% and 8.00% is only 3.20 percentage points. That's not a lot of cushion to cover the risk of owning real estate. So sponsors right now are being forced to offer higher preferred returns, accept lower promotes, or bring better deals to the table to attract LP capital.

3. Why a professional cares

The promote is how a GP actually gets rich — not from a salary, but from that outsized share of profits above the hurdle. When Treasury rates rise, the hurdle LPs demand rises with it, which means the GP has to perform at a higher level just to reach their promote. In a deal pitched to investors today, a GP who structures the waterfall wrong — too low a preferred return, too rich a promote — will lose the LP to a Treasury bond.

Check yourself

A GP raises $5 million from an LP and promises an 8% preferred return before the GP earns any promote. The deal closes, and one year later the property generates $350,000 of distributable cash flow (money available to pay investors after all expenses and debt payments).

Does the GP earn any promote this year? Show your reasoning with the numbers.

Show answer

No. The 8% preferred return on $5 million is $5,000,000 × 0.08 = $400,000. The property only generated $350,000 — $50,000 short of the hurdle — so all $350,000 goes to the LP, and the GP earns no promote this year because the LP's preferred return was never fully paid.

Review

Absorption (brief #8): A new 200,000-square-foot office building opens in Dallas. Over the next quarter, tenants sign leases for 120,000 square feet — but two other tenants in the same market vacate 150,000 square feet of older space. What is the net absorption for that quarter, and what does the sign of that number tell you about the market?

Show answer

Net absorption is the total space newly occupied minus the total space newly vacated: 120,000 − 150,000 = negative 30,000 square feet. A negative number means the market is giving back space faster than it's absorbing new space — a signal of oversupply or weakening demand, which typically pushes rents down.

The capital stack (brief #13): A developer's deal goes sideways and the property sells at a loss. List the four layers of the capital stack in the order they get paid back, and name which layer is most likely to lose everything.

Show answer

The payback order is: (1) senior debt — the main mortgage, first in line and most protected; (2) mezzanine debt — a second loan that sits below senior debt; (3) preferred equity — investors with a priority return but no loan; (4) common equity — the sponsor and last-in investors. Common equity is most likely to lose everything because they are paid last, and in a loss scenario there is often nothing left by the time the first three layers are satisfied.
