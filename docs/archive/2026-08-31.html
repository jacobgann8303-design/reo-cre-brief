Today's concept: Basis point

1. The everyday version

Imagine your savings account pays 2.00% interest. The bank raises it to 2.25%. That's a quarter of one percent — a small move, but on $10,000, it's the difference between earning $200 a year and $225. When you're talking about tiny percentages, saying "a quarter of a percent" gets clunky fast. So finance people invented a shorthand: one basis point equals one one-hundredth of one percent (0.01%). That quarter-percent move? That's 25 basis points — abbreviated "bps" and said out loud as "bips."

2. The real version

This morning, the 10-Year Treasury — the interest rate the U.S. government pays to borrow money for ten years, which acts as the floor that CRE investors price everything else above — sits at 4.67%. Last Thursday it was 4.66%. That move:

4.67% − 4.66% = 0.01% = 1 basis point

One basis point sounds trivial. But apply it to a $10,000,000 apartment deal. A cap rate (the ratio of a property's annual income to its price) of 5.67% on that building implies $567,000 in annual income — that's $10,000,000 × 5.67%. If rates push buyers to demand 5.68% instead, the same $567,000 of income now implies a price of $567,000 ÷ 0.0568 = $9,982,394 — a drop of roughly $17,600 on one basis point alone.

3. Why a professional cares

A lender quoting a loan at "Treasury plus 200 bps" is saying: whatever the 10-year is today, add 2.00% on top — so at today's 4.67%, that loan costs 6.67%. If the 10-year moves up 25 bps overnight, that same loan formula now costs 6.92%, which may be the difference between a deal penciling out (generating enough income to cover costs) and a deal dying.

Review

Brief #5 concept — The 10-year Treasury and cap rate spread: Here's the setup — don't just define the terms, reason through the chain. If the Fed hikes rates in September and the 10-year climbs from 4.67% to 5.00%, what pressure does that put on property prices, and through what mechanism? (A cap rate is the property's annual income divided by its price. A spread is the gap between the cap rate and the 10-year.)

Show answer

When the 10-year rises, investors demand a higher cap rate to maintain the same spread above the "safe" Treasury return — and a higher cap rate on the same income means a lower price (because Price = Income ÷ Cap Rate). So rising rates mechanically push property values down unless rents rise fast enough to offset the higher cap rate demanded.
