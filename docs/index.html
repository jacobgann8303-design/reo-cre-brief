Today's concept: The capital stack

1. The everyday version

Imagine you and two friends are buying a $20,000 used car together. You put in $4,000 of your own cash. Your parents lend you $14,000 and get paid back first if anything goes wrong — they're the safest. Your friend chips in $2,000 but only gets paid after your parents do, so they're taking more risk. That layered arrangement — who gets paid first, who eats losses first — is the capital stack.

2. The real version

A developer buys an apartment building for $10 million. They don't pay cash for all of it. Here's how the money is typically layered:

| Layer | Amount | Who it is |

|---|---|---|

| Senior debt (safest — first to get paid back) | $6,500,000 | Bank or CMBS lender |

| Mezzanine debt (middle — paid after senior) | $1,500,000 | Private lender |

| Equity (riskiest — last to get paid, first to lose) | $2,000,000 | The developer / investors |

$6,500,000 + $1,500,000 + $2,000,000 = $10,000,000

Today the 10-year Treasury is at 4.98%. The senior lender will price their loan above that — say, 7.5%. That rate is applied to $6.5 million: $6,500,000 × 0.075 = $487,500 per year in interest, before the mezzanine lender or the equity investors see a dollar. The equity at the bottom of the stack only gets whatever is left over after every lender above them is paid.

3. Why a professional cares

The higher interest rates climb — and today's 10-year at 4.98% is high relative to the past decade — the more the top of the stack costs, which squeezes or wipes out the return at the bottom. That's precisely why Trepp found 75% of office loans can't refinance right now: the new debt payments at today's rates would consume all the income the building generates, leaving nothing for equity and no margin for the lender.

Check yourself

Question: A building generates $650,000 per year in NOI (Net Operating Income — total rental income minus operating expenses, before debt payments). The senior loan is $6,000,000 at 7% interest, and the mezzanine loan is $1,000,000 at 11% interest. After paying both lenders' annual interest, how much is left for the equity investors? Is there anything left at all?

Show answer

Senior interest = $6,000,000 × 0.07 = $420,000. Mezzanine interest = $1,000,000 × 0.11 = $110,000. Total debt service = $530,000. Cash left for equity = $650,000 − $530,000 = $120,000. Yes, there is something left, but just barely — and if NOI dropped even 20%, the equity investors would get nothing and the mezzanine lender might start taking losses too.

Review

Price per square foot (Brief #49): You're looking at two office buildings in the same neighborhood. Building A sold for $400 per square foot; Building B sold for $280 per square foot. A broker tells you Building A is obviously the better deal because it's newer. What is one thing price per square foot cannot tell you on its own that you'd need to know before agreeing with the broker?

Show answer

Price per square foot tells you what buyers paid per unit of space, but it doesn't tell you how much income the building produces — a newer building might have lower rents, higher vacancy, or higher operating costs, meaning the lower price-per-foot of Building B could actually reflect a better income-producing asset. You need NOI or cap rate to judge value, not just price per foot.

Escalations and CPI bumps (Brief #24): You sign a 5-year office lease at $30 per square foot per year with a "CPI bump" — meaning rent increases each year by however much the Consumer Price Index (the government's measure of inflation) rose. If CPI rises 4% in year one, what do you pay in year two, and why does a landlord specifically want this clause rather than a fixed rent?

Show answer

Year two rent = $30 × 1.04 = $31.20 per square foot. The landlord wants a CPI bump because inflation erodes purchasing power — if costs rise 4% but rent stays flat, the landlord is effectively earning less in real terms each year; the escalation clause keeps income roughly in step with the economy and protects the building's NOI over time.
