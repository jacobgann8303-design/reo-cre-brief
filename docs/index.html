Today's concept: Submarket

1. The everyday version

Imagine you're looking for an apartment in Lubbock. "Lubbock" is too broad to tell you anything useful — rent near Texas Tech is completely different from rent out by the airport or near the South Loop. So you naturally think in neighborhoods: Tech Terrace, Canyon Lakes, Wolfforth. Those are your submarkets. Same city, same general economy, totally different pricing and demand.

2. The real version

A submarket is a defined geographic slice within a metro area where supply, demand, vacancy, and rents behave differently from the metro average. Professionals use them because averages lie.

Here's why it matters with a real number: the HPI groundbreaking today is in North Austin near The Domain — not "Austin." That distinction is everything. Austin's overall apartment vacancy might be 8%, but The Domain submarket could be at 4% because it's surrounded by tech employers. If a developer underwrites (evaluates the financials of) a deal using the citywide 8% vacancy instead of the submarket's 4%, they'd assume slower lease-up and undervalue the project. The arithmetic: if a 396-unit building fills at 4% vacancy instead of 8%, that's roughly 16 more occupied units (396 × 0.04 = 15.8). At $1,800/month each, that's about $28,800 more in monthly revenue — a number that absolutely changes your return.

396 × 0.04 = ~16 units

16 × $1,800 = $28,800/month

3. Why a professional cares

Lenders and investors underwrite at the submarket level, not the metro level — a broker who pitches a deal using metro averages when the submarket is tighter will get caught immediately. Knowing the right submarket number is the difference between a credible underwriting model and one that gets laughed out of the room.

Check yourself

A new 300-unit apartment building is proposed in a metro where citywide vacancy is 9%. But the specific submarket has vacancy of 3%. An investor uses the 9% metro figure to project stabilized occupancy (the point at which a building reaches its normal, steady rental rate). How does that mistake affect their projected NOI — net operating income, meaning the income left after operating expenses but before debt payments — compared to reality, and in which direction?

Show answer

Using 9% vacancy instead of 3% means the investor assumes 27 units empty (300 × 0.09) when really only 9 are empty (300 × 0.03) — 18 more units occupied than modeled; the real NOI will be higher than projected, so the investor undervalued the asset and may have passed on or underbid a good deal.

Review

1031 exchange (taught in brief #32): You own a warehouse you bought for $500,000 that's now worth $1,200,000. You want to sell it and buy an apartment building instead. What is a 1031 exchange, and what is the main thing it lets you avoid — at least temporarily?

Show answer

A 1031 exchange is an IRS rule that lets you sell an investment property and roll all the proceeds into a new "like-kind" investment property without paying capital gains tax at the time of the sale — you defer (delay, not eliminate) the tax until you eventually sell the replacement property without doing another exchange.

Equity waterfall and promote (taught in brief #14): A real estate deal has outside investors (LPs — limited partners, the people who put in capital but don't run the deal) and a sponsor (the GP — general partner, the person who finds and manages the deal). Once the LPs get their money back plus an 8% annual return, the remaining profits split 70% to LPs and 30% to the GP. What is the GP's extra 30% slice called, and why does the GP get it even if they put in little of their own money?

Show answer

That extra slice is called the promote (or carried interest) — the GP earns it as compensation for finding the deal, managing it, and taking on execution risk, not just for putting up capital; it's the sponsor's performance fee built into the structure of the deal.
