Today's concept: Pro forma

1. The everyday version

Say you're thinking about buying a used car to drive for Uber on weekends. Before you spend the money, you sit down and estimate: you'll probably do 15 rides a week at $12 each — that's $180 in revenue. Gas and insurance will cost about $60. So you'd clear roughly $120 a week. You haven't driven a single ride yet. That whole estimate is a pro forma — a projection of what you *expect* to happen, written down before the fact.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, a pro forma is a spreadsheet that projects a property's income and expenses before you buy it. Say you're looking at a small apartment building. You write down:

  • 10 units × $1,400/month rent = $168,000/year in potential revenue
  • You assume 5% of units are empty on average (that's called a vacancy assumption — the percentage of units you expect to go un-rented): 5% × $168,000 = $8,400 in lost income
  • Operating expenses (maintenance, taxes, insurance, management): $55,000/year

So your projected NOI (Net Operating Income — what's left after expenses but before your mortgage payment) = $168,000 − $8,400 − $55,000 = $104,600.

Now, with the 10-year Treasury at 5.07% today, buyers in this market are demanding higher returns than they were two years ago. If the market cap rate (the return buyers expect — NOI ÷ purchase price) is 6.5%, then:

$104,600 ÷ 0.065 = ~$1,609,000 — that's the price the pro forma implies you should pay.

Change your vacancy assumption from 5% to 10% and your NOI drops to $96,200, which at the same 6.5% cap rate implies a price of only ~$1,480,000. One assumption, $129,000 difference.

3. Why a professional cares

Lenders and investors scrutinize pro forma assumptions because that's where deals get oversold — an aggressive rent assumption or an unrealistically low vacancy rate can make a bad deal look good on paper. When rates are high, like now, the margin for error on a pro forma shrinks fast.

Check yourself

You're underwriting a small office building. Your pro forma shows $200,000 in potential rent, a 10% vacancy assumption, and $70,000 in operating expenses. What is the projected NOI? If a buyer wants a 7.0% cap rate, what price does that imply?

Show answer

Potential rent minus vacancy ($200,000 × 10% = $20,000 lost) = $180,000 effective income; minus $70,000 expenses = $110,000 NOI. At a 7.0% cap rate: $110,000 ÷ 0.07 = $1,571,429 implied price.

Review

Vacancy vs. availability (Brief #53): A building has 100,000 square feet. 10,000 square feet have no tenant and are sitting empty. Another 15,000 square feet have a tenant paying rent, but that tenant has announced they're leaving in six months and the landlord is already marketing the space. What is the vacancy rate, and what is the availability rate — and why are they different numbers?

Show answer

Vacancy rate = 10,000 ÷ 100,000 = 10% — only the space that is physically empty right now with no tenant paying rent. Availability rate = 25,000 ÷ 100,000 = 25% — it includes both the empty space AND the occupied-but-soon-to-be-available space, because a tenant searching for space today would consider both options.

Supply pipeline (Brief #42): Today's headlines say apartment deliveries fell 27% to 442,000 units nationally, and occupancy is at 95.7%. Without knowing the exact pipeline chart, explain what a shrinking construction pipeline generally means for vacancy and rents over the next one to two years — and why.

Show answer

A shrinking pipeline means fewer new units will be delivered, so less new supply will compete for tenants; with demand holding steady, that tends to push vacancy down and give landlords more pricing power to raise rents — which is consistent with the 95.7% occupancy number already showing up today.
