Today's concept: Going-in vs. exit cap rate

1. The everyday version

Imagine you buy a used car for $10,000 to use as a rideshare vehicle. On day one, based on the routes you plan to run, you expect to clear $1,000 a year in profit after gas and maintenance. That's a 10% return on what you paid. Five years later, you sell the car. The buyer looks at the same car — now older, in a different market, with different rideshare competition — and only expects to clear $800 a year from it. He'll pay less than you did, because his expected return on *his* purchase price works out differently. The return you underwrote when you *bought* is different from the return someone else underwrites when you *sell*.

2. The real version

A cap rate (capitalization rate — the ratio of a property's annual net operating income to its purchase price) is how CRE investors measure return on a property. NOI (Net Operating Income — rent collected minus operating expenses, before debt payments) divided by purchase price equals the cap rate.

Say you buy an apartment building today for $2,000,000. Its NOI is $140,000.

> $140,000 ÷ $2,000,000 = 7.0% going-in cap rate

That 7.0% is your going-in cap rate — the return implied by what you paid on day one.

Now you hold the property five years and go to sell. By then, the 10-year Treasury is still elevated (it's 5.10% right now, which is high by recent history). Buyers demand a bigger cushion above Treasuries to justify the extra risk of owning real estate. So buyers in your exit market will only underwrite a 7.75% cap rate. If your NOI has grown to $150,000 by then:

> $150,000 ÷ 0.0775 = $1,935,484 exit price

You bought for $2,000,000 and sold for $1,935,484 — a loss on the asset itself, even though NOI grew. That gap between 7.0% going-in and 7.75% exit is called cap rate expansion (buyers accepting a smaller price for each dollar of income, i.e., demanding a higher return). The Bisnow story today about private equity lagging REITs in marking portfolios to market is exactly this problem playing out at scale.

3. Why a professional cares

If you model a 7.0% exit cap rate when buying today but the market exits at 7.75%, a deal that looked profitable on paper destroys equity. Underwriting the exit cap rate conservatively — especially when the 10-year is high — is often the difference between a deal that works and one that doesn't.

Check yourself

You're buying a small retail strip center for $1,500,000. Its NOI is $90,000. You plan to sell in four years. At exit, you project NOI will have grown to $97,500. If buyers at exit demand a 7.0% cap rate, what is your projected exit price — and is it higher or lower than what you paid? Show the arithmetic.

Show answer

Going-in cap rate = $90,000 ÷ $1,500,000 = 6.0%. Exit price = $97,500 ÷ 0.07 = $1,392,857. That is lower than the $1,500,000 purchase price, because even though NOI grew, the exit cap rate expanded from 6.0% to 7.0% — buyers demanded a higher return at sale — which compressed the price they were willing to pay.

Review

Basis points (Brief #52): The 10-year Treasury moved from 5.12% to 5.10% — a drop of 2 basis points. Without looking anything up: what is a basis point, and why do professionals use that unit instead of just saying "it dropped 0.02%"?

Show answer

A basis point is one one-hundredth of a percentage point (0.01%), so 2 basis points = 0.02%. Professionals use basis points because "percent" is ambiguous when you're already talking about a percentage — saying a rate moved "0.02 percent" could be misread as 0.02% of the rate itself rather than an absolute change, and in markets where tiny moves carry large dollar consequences, precision matters.

Lease rollover risk and the maturity wall (Brief #26): A landlord owns a 200,000 SF office building. Leases on 160,000 SF of it all expire in the same year. What risk does that create, and what's the connection to today's office CMBS headlines?

Show answer

When a large share of leases expire at once, the landlord faces rollover risk — the possibility that tenants don't renew, leaving the building partially or fully vacant right when the mortgage may also need to be refinanced; this concentration of lease expirations is sometimes called a maturity wall. Today's office CMBS distress stories (101 Hudson Street, 123 William Street) reflect exactly this: buildings where tenants didn't renew, income fell, and the loan came due at the same time the property couldn't support its debt.
