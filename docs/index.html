Today's concept: The 10-year Treasury and cap rate spread

1. The everyday version

Imagine you have two ways to lend your friend money. Option A: lend it to your most reliable friend — zero chance they stiff you — and they'll pay you back 5% interest. Option B: lend it to a friend who's a little riskier — maybe they'll repay you, maybe not — so they have to offer you 7% to make it worth the extra worry. That extra 2% you demand for taking on more risk? That gap is called a spread. The riskier the borrower, the wider the spread needs to be before you'll say yes.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, the "reliable friend" is the U.S. government. When you buy a 10-year Treasury bond — you're lending money to the federal government for ten years — you earn a safe, guaranteed return. Today that rate is 4.94%.

Now say you're considering buying an apartment building instead. That's riskier than lending to the government — tenants can leave, roofs can leak, markets can soften. So you demand a higher return. That higher return is measured by the cap rate (capitalization rate — the building's annual net income divided by its purchase price, expressed as a percent; it's the building's yield before any debt).

Professionals typically expect a spread of roughly 150 to 250 basis points above the 10-year Treasury. So right now:

4.94% (Treasury) + 2.00% (typical spread) = ~6.94% minimum cap rate before many buyers will seriously consider a deal.

The Walker & Dunlop Texas refinancing in today's headlines — $117.3M across 960 apartment units — works out to roughly $122,000 per unit. If those properties were trading at a 6.5% cap rate, that spread over today's Treasury would be only about 156 bps — thin, but inside the historical range. If rates rise and the Treasury hits 5.5%, that same spread logic would push required cap rates toward 7.5%, meaning prices would have to fall to make the math work.

3. Why a professional cares

When the 10-year Treasury rises, the spread math forces cap rates up — and because cap rate and price move in opposite directions (higher cap rate = lower price for the same income), rising rates push property values down. A broker, a lender, or an investor watches the 10-year every morning because it tells them whether the deals on their desk are still priced correctly — or whether yesterday's agreed price is already underwater.

Check yourself

Question: A small office building generates $200,000 per year in net operating income (NOI — the income left after paying operating expenses but before paying the mortgage). It's listed for sale at $2,500,000. The 10-year Treasury is at 4.94%. A buyer expects a spread of at least 200 basis points above the Treasury. Does this deal clear the buyer's minimum? Show your math.

Show answer

The cap rate on this deal is $200,000 ÷ $2,500,000 = 8.0%. The buyer's minimum required cap rate is 4.94% + 2.00% = 6.94%. Because 8.0% exceeds 6.94%, the deal clears the buyer's hurdle — they're being compensated more than their minimum required spread, so the price is attractive relative to today's Treasury rate.

Review

Supply pipeline (Brief #42): When you look at a construction pipeline chart for a city — say it shows 5,000 new apartment units under construction set to deliver over the next 18 months — what is the main risk that number represents for existing landlords, and what would you look for on the same chart to judge how serious that risk is?

Show answer

The risk is oversupply — all those new units hitting the market at once increases competition for tenants, which can push rents down or push vacancy (the share of empty units) up for existing buildings. To judge severity, you'd compare the new supply figure against current absorption — the rate at which tenants are actually filling units — to see whether demand is growing fast enough to absorb the new inventory without hurting existing landlords.

Opportunity zones (Brief #33): A developer is looking at two identical plots of land in the same city. One is inside a federally designated Opportunity Zone and one is not. What is the core tax benefit that makes the Opportunity Zone site potentially more attractive, and what is one thing that benefit cannot fix?

Show answer

The core benefit is a deferral and potential reduction of capital gains taxes — investors who put realized gains into a Qualified Opportunity Fund can postpone and possibly reduce what they owe the IRS, and gains earned inside the zone after 10 years can be tax-free. But today's headlines noted that Opportunity Zones can help finish projects, not start them — the tax benefit cannot overcome fundamental deal-killers like construction costs that make a project unprofitable before the first shovel hits the ground.
