Today's concept: The four asset classes

1. The everyday version

Think about the different ways people make money from property in your town. One person owns an apartment complex and collects rent from families. Another owns the strip mall where you get your haircut. A third owns the warehouse where Amazon stores packages before delivery. A fourth owns the downtown office building where law firms lease space. Same basic idea — own a building, collect rent — but four completely different businesses with different customers, different risks, and different rhythms.

2. The real version

Commercial real estate is organized around those four buckets. Professionals call them the four major asset classes (an asset class is just a category of investment that behaves similarly):

  • Multifamily — apartment buildings where people live. MAA, your tracked REIT, is a pure multifamily company. Its stock closed at $118.38 today.
  • Office — buildings where companies lease workspace. HALL Group just bought 112,000 SF of it in Uptown Dallas this morning.
  • Retail — shopping centers, strip malls, grocery-anchored centers. Nuveen just paid $445.5M for 13 of them across the West Coast.
  • Industrial — warehouses, distribution centers, manufacturing buildings. Brookfield and CPP just completed a $5.2B buyout of LXP Industrial Trust, which owns 107 distribution buildings across the Sun Belt and Midwest.

Each asset class has its own typical cap rate (the income a property produces divided by its price — $168,000 ÷ $2,400,000 = 7.0%, for example), its own lease structure, its own tenant type, and its own sensitivity to the economy.

Here's the shape that matters right now: the 10-year Treasury is at 4.98%. Industrial cap rates in strong markets have been trading around 5–6%. That spread — the gap between what a "safe" government bond pays and what real estate yields — is thin. When the spread is thin, buyers have less cushion and deals get harder to pencil.

3. Why a professional cares

A lender, broker, or investor who doesn't immediately know which asset class they're looking at can't begin to underwrite a deal — the rent structure, vacancy risk, loan terms, and buyer pool are all different. When the Fed signals more rate hikes (as two Fed presidents did this week), the first question every CRE professional asks is: which asset class gets hit first, and which can pass higher costs through to tenants fast enough to survive?

Check yourself

You're comparing two deals. Deal A is an industrial warehouse in Dallas that sold for $10,000,000 and produces $600,000 in NOI (Net Operating Income — the rent collected minus operating expenses, before debt payments) per year. Deal B is a multifamily apartment complex that sold for $10,000,000 and produces $500,000 in NOI per year. Both are the same price. Which one has the higher cap rate, and what does that tell you about how the market is pricing the two asset classes relative to each other?

Show answer

Deal A has a cap rate of 6.0% ($600,000 ÷ $10,000,000), and Deal B has a cap rate of 5.0% ($500,000 ÷ $10,000,000); the higher cap rate on the industrial deal means buyers demanded a bigger return to own it — or equivalently, the market priced the multifamily asset more expensively relative to its income, likely because apartment demand and rent growth were seen as more predictable.

Review

Due diligence period (Brief #20): You're under contract to buy a $3M office building. Your attorney mentions you're still in the "due diligence period." Without using that phrase, what is actually happening during this stretch of time — what are you doing, and what right does it give you?

Show answer

During due diligence, the buyer is inspecting the property and reviewing everything that affects its value — leases, financials, environmental reports, title, physical condition — and typically has the contractual right to walk away and get their deposit back if something turns up that they don't like, before that window closes and the deal becomes binding.

1031 exchange (Brief #32): A developer sells an industrial building in Houston for a $2M profit. Her accountant mentions a "1031." What is the basic deal the tax code is offering her, and what's the catch?

Show answer

A 1031 exchange (named for Section 1031 of the tax code) lets her defer — not eliminate — the capital gains tax on that $2M profit as long as she reinvests the proceeds into another "like-kind" property within strict time limits (45 days to identify the replacement, 180 days to close); the catch is that if she ever sells without doing another 1031, all the deferred tax comes due.
