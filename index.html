Today's concept: Development spread

1. The everyday version

Imagine you can borrow money from your parents at 4% interest to flip furniture — you buy cheap chairs, refinish them, and sell them for more. Before you start, you estimate you can earn a 9% return on what you put in. The gap between what you earn (9%) and what you owe (4%) is your cushion. If that cushion is wide, you do the project. If your parents raise their rate to 8% and your return stays at 9%, the cushion nearly disappears — a small miscalculation wipes you out. You might just not do the project.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, developers do the same math. They estimate what return a completed building will generate — this is called the development yield, sometimes called the going-in yield on cost: the property's expected annual income (NOI — net operating income, meaning rent collected minus operating expenses) divided by the total cost to build.

Then they compare that to the cap rate (capitalization rate) at which similar completed buildings are selling in the market. Cap rate = NOI ÷ sale price — it's the market's agreed-upon return for owning that type of building. If the market cap rate is 6.5% and your development yield is 8.5%, the spread (the gap) is 200 basis points, or 2.00%.

Here's the arithmetic with round numbers:

  • You spend $50 million to build an apartment complex.
  • At stabilization (full occupancy), it generates $4.25 million in NOI.
  • Development yield: $4,250,000 ÷ $50,000,000 = 8.5%
  • The market sells comparable buildings at a 6.5% cap rate, meaning a buyer would pay $4,250,000 ÷ 0.065 = $65.4 million for it.
  • Spread: 8.5% − 6.5% = 200 bps. That spread is your profit margin baked into the structure.

Now look at today: the 10-year Treasury is at 5.22%. Construction loans are typically priced as a spread above the 10-year, so borrowing costs are elevated. If your construction costs rise and your development yield compresses to, say, 7.0%, but market cap rates have also risen to 6.8% because buyers demand more return in a high-rate world, your spread collapses to just 20 bps — barely enough to justify the risk.

3. Why a professional cares

A developer uses the development spread to decide whether to break ground at all — if the spread is too thin (under roughly 100–150 bps in most markets), the risk of cost overruns or a slower lease-up isn't worth it. A lender uses it to judge whether the loan is safe: if the spread is wide, the borrower has room to absorb problems before the deal goes underwater.

Check yourself

A developer plans to build a small office building. Total cost: $20 million. Expected annual NOI at stabilization: $1.4 million. Market cap rate for comparable office buildings: 6.5%. What is the development spread, and does it suggest this project has a healthy margin?

Show answer

Development yield = $1,400,000 ÷ $20,000,000 = 7.0%. Spread = 7.0% − 6.5% = 50 basis points. That is a thin margin — most developers want at least 100–150 bps to cover cost overruns and lease-up risk, so this project is borderline at best and would likely get a hard look before anyone broke ground.

Review

Free rent and effective rent (Brief #23): A landlord advertises office space at $30 per square foot per year and offers a tenant six months of free rent on a five-year lease. What is the effective rent — the rent that actually reflects what the landlord collects — and why does it matter that the headline rate is $30?

Show answer

Over five years the tenant pays full rent for 4.5 years: $30 × 4.5 ÷ 5 = $27 per square foot per year effective rent. The headline rate of $30 overstates what the landlord actually earns, which matters because a buyer pricing the building on $30 NOI would overpay — the real income is lower.

REIT structure (Brief #30): A REIT (Real Estate Investment Trust — a company that owns income-producing property and trades like a stock) is required by law to distribute at least 90% of its taxable income to shareholders each year. Why does that rule make it hard for a REIT to grow by reinvesting its own profits?

Show answer

Because it must pay out 90% of taxable income as dividends, a REIT can only keep up to 10% of earnings internally — it can't quietly stockpile cash to fund new acquisitions the way a regular company can. To grow, it almost always has to raise new money by issuing new shares or borrowing, which is why REITs watch the capital markets so closely.
