Today's concept: Broker vs. principal vs. advisor

1. The everyday version

Imagine you're selling a used car. You could hire someone at CarMax to find you a buyer — they get a fee when the deal closes, and they don't care which car sells as long as *a* car sells. That's a broker: they facilitate a deal between two other people and earn a commission. Or you could be the one actually buying the car with your own money, taking on the risk yourself — that's acting as a principal: you own it, you're on the hook for it. Or you could pay a mechanic to inspect the car and tell you whether the price is fair, with no stake in whether you buy or not — that's an advisor: they give you an opinion for a fee, but they don't touch the money.

2. The real version

These same three roles exist in every CRE deal, and knowing which hat someone is wearing tells you whose interests they're actually serving.

A broker — say, a CBRE agent — represents a buyer or seller (or sometimes both, called dual agency) and earns a commission when the transaction closes, typically 1–2% of the sale price. No deal, no pay. That creates an incentive to close, not necessarily to get you the best terms.

A principal owns the asset directly and takes real risk. When Blackstone took out that $1.3 billion CMBS loan (CMBS = Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities — a loan that gets bundled with other loans and sold to investors on the bond market) on an apartment portfolio, Blackstone was acting as principal: they owned the buildings, they signed the loan, and now they're the ones in special servicing (the workout process when a loan goes sideways). If the buildings lose value, Blackstone loses money.

An advisor — like a consulting arm of JLL or CBRE — gets paid a flat fee or retainer to tell a pension fund whether to buy, how to underwrite the deal (underwrite = run the numbers to decide if the risk is worth taking), and what the market looks like. They earn the same fee whether the deal happens or not.

Here's why it matters with a real number: MAA closed at $119.62 today, up 2.56%. If a broker pitches you on buying MAA shares through a real estate fund, they make a commission. If MAA itself deploys capital to buy a new apartment complex, they're acting as principal — their own shareholders bear the risk. If a research firm tells you "MAA is fairly valued at this price," they're advising. Same asset, three completely different incentive structures.

3. Why a professional cares

When you're in a deal room, the first thing you want to know about every person at the table is: *are they a broker, a principal, or an advisor?* A broker's opinion about whether a price is fair is worth less than an advisor's, because the broker doesn't get paid unless you agree to the price. Lenders, institutional buyers, and regulators all evaluate advice differently based on who's giving it and what they earn from being right — or wrong.

Check yourself

You're a Texas pension fund considering buying a $50 million apartment complex in Lubbock. The seller's broker tells you the building is worth every penny and the rent growth story is strong. An independent advisor you hired separately says the rent growth assumptions are too aggressive given the concession data. Who do you trust more, and why? What would it take for the broker's opinion to become more credible?

Show answer

Trust the advisor more — the broker only gets paid if the deal closes, so they have a financial incentive to keep you enthusiastic; the advisor gets a flat fee regardless of outcome, so their opinion isn't distorted by the transaction. The broker's opinion becomes more credible if they have a track record of deals in that specific submarket, if they're providing audited comps rather than projections, or — best of all — if they have their own capital in a similar deal, making them act more like a principal who has skin in the game.

Review

Development spread (Brief #36): A developer is building an apartment complex. When it's finished, the market cap rate (the return buyers in the open market expect — calculated as net operating income divided by purchase price) for similar buildings is 5.5%. The developer's yield on cost (the NOI the building will produce divided by everything it cost to build, including land and construction) is 7.2%. What is the development spread, and what does it tell you?

Show answer

The development spread is 7.2% minus 5.5% = 1.7 percentage points — that gap is where the developer's profit lives; it means they built something worth more than it cost, because a buyer using a 5.5% cap rate would pay a price that implies a lower return than the developer actually achieved.

Construction loan draw schedule (Brief #37): A developer has a $10 million construction loan. Rather than handing over the full $10 million on day one, the lender releases money in stages as work is completed. Why does the lender structure it this way instead of funding everything upfront?

Show answer

The lender releases draws in stages to limit their exposure — if the developer stops building halfway through, the lender has only funded the work that actually exists as collateral, not the full loan amount; it also keeps the developer accountable, because they have to prove progress to access each tranche of money.
