Today's concept: Preferred equity vs. mezzanine debt

1. The everyday version

Imagine three friends buying a used car together for $10,000. Friend A puts in $6,000 and says, "I want my money back first no matter what, plus a guaranteed $300 a year — but I don't get to vote on decisions." Friend B puts in $2,000 and says, "I want my money back second, and I get a higher return to make up for the extra risk — and if you miss a payment to me, I can actually take the car." Friend C puts in $2,000 last, gets whatever's left over, and has all the control.

Friend B is behaving like a mezzanine lender — technically owed money, has enforcement rights, can take the asset. Friend A is behaving like a preferred equity investor — gets paid before Friend C, but has softer legal tools if things go wrong.

2. The real version

In commercial real estate, most deals are funded in layers — professionals call this the capital stack (picture a literal stack of claims on the same building, ordered by who gets paid first). From bottom to top:

| Layer | Who | Gets paid | Risk |

|---|---|---|---|

| Senior debt | Bank or CMBS lender | First | Lowest |

| Mezzanine debt | Mezz lender | Second | Medium-high |

| Preferred equity | Pref equity investor | Third | High |

| Common equity | Sponsor / owner | Last | Highest |

Here's a simplified deal at today's numbers. A developer is buying an apartment building for $20,000,000.

  • Senior loan (the bank): $13,000,000 at ~7.5% (roughly the 10-year at 5.21% plus a spread of ~230 bps — a spread is the extra percentage points a lender charges above the risk-free Treasury rate to compensate for the risk of lending on real estate).
  • Mezzanine debt: $3,000,000 at 12%. The mezz lender is a *lender* — the developer *owes* this money. If the developer misses payments, the mezz lender can foreclose on the developer's ownership stake and take control of the deal. That threat is the lever.
  • Preferred equity: $2,000,000 at 10%. The pref equity investor *owns a slice* of the entity that owns the building — they're not technically owed a loan. They get a preferred (meaning "paid before the common owner") return of $200,000/year: $2,000,000 × 10% = $200,000. But if payments stop, their remedy is usually governance rights — voting the sponsor out, blocking distributions — not foreclosure.
  • Common equity (developer): $2,000,000 — gets everything left after everyone above is paid.

The key mechanical difference: mezzanine debt = a loan with foreclosure rights. Preferred equity = an ownership position with priority economics but weaker enforcement.

3. Why a professional cares

Right now, with the 10-year at 5.21% and PIMCO warning it could hit 6%, senior lenders are tightening — they'll loan less against the same building. That gap between what the bank will lend and what the deal costs has to be filled somehow. Preferred equity and mezzanine debt are exactly what fills that gap, which is why GlobeSt's headline about refinancing distress matters: owners who can't refinance at the same senior loan size as 2021 are going back to the mezz and pref equity markets right now, and those providers are charging 12–15% because they can. The difference between mezz and pref equity determines who controls the building if the deal goes sideways — that is a billion-dollar distinction when distress hits.

Check yourself

A developer's $18M apartment deal has a $11M senior loan, a $2M mezzanine loan at 13%, and $2M of preferred equity at 11%. The building's NOI (Net Operating Income — annual rent collected minus operating expenses, before debt payments) drops and the developer stops making payments on both. The mezz lender and the pref equity investor both want the building. Who has the stronger legal position to actually take control, and roughly how much does each party collect annually when payments are flowing normally?

Show answer

The mezzanine lender has the stronger legal position — mezz debt is a loan secured by the developer's ownership interest, so the mezz lender can foreclose on that interest and take control of the building if payments stop; the preferred equity investor only has contractual and governance rights within the ownership entity, not true foreclosure power. When payments are flowing, the mezz lender collects $2,000,000 × 13% = $260,000/year and the pref equity investor collects $2,000,000 × 11% = $220,000/year.

Review

FFO and AFFO (Brief #31): A REIT (Real Estate Investment Trust — a company that owns income-producing real estate and trades like a stock) owns a $50M apartment complex. Standard accounting requires it to record $2M of depreciation (a paper write-down of the building's value each year) as an expense, making net income look lower than the actual cash the company generated. Why do analysts use FFO instead of net income to measure a REIT's performance, and what does AFFO adjust for on top of that?

Show answer

Depreciation is a non-cash accounting expense — the building isn't actually losing $2M in real value every year the way a car does — so subtracting it makes net income misleading for REITs; FFO (Funds From Operations) adds depreciation back to net income to show the cash the business actually produced. AFFO (Adjusted FFO) goes one step further by also subtracting recurring capital expenditures — money the REIT must actually spend to maintain the buildings — giving an even cleaner picture of sustainable cash available to pay dividends.

Anchor tenant and co-tenancy clauses (Brief #25): A strip mall has a lease with a regional grocery chain (the anchor tenant — the large, well-known tenant whose traffic draws customers to smaller shops around it). Buried in the leases of three smaller tenants is a co-tenancy clause. The grocery chain closes its location. What does a co-tenancy clause likely allow those smaller tenants to do, and why does that make the grocery closure so damaging to the landlord beyond just losing one rent check?

Show answer

A co-tenancy clause gives smaller tenants the right to pay reduced rent — or sometimes exit their leases entirely — if a named anchor tenant closes, because their business depends on the foot traffic that anchor generates. The landlord doesn't just lose the anchor's rent; they risk triggering rent cuts or vacancies across multiple smaller tenants simultaneously, which can collapse the property's NOI and its value in a chain reaction.
